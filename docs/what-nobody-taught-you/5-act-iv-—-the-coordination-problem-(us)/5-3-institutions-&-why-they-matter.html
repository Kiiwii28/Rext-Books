5-3 Institutions & why they matter

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Most of Act IV concerns people failing to get what they want together. This subsection is about the machinery that can make the difference: the rules, conventions, and enforcement arrangements under which coordination either happens or doesn't. Institutions are the least visible variable in most arguments about why some places, firms, and communities work and others don't — and the most load-bearing.

What an institution is, and what it isn't

Douglass North gave the definition that most of the social sciences now work from: institutions are "the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction." Rules of the game, in his famous phrase. More precisely, an institution is a stable, shared, enforced expectation about behaviour — a rule plus the machinery that makes it stick. Money, property, marriage, the queue, peer review, limited liability, the standard metre, and the informal rule that you don't take the last coffee without making more are all institutions in this sense.

Two clarifications prevent most confusion about the topic.

Institutions are not organizations. A hospital, a bank, a university, and a ministry are organizations — groups of people bound by a common purpose. The rules that govern them, constrain them, and let them hold property and make contracts are institutions. The distinction sounds pedantic and isn't: it is the difference between changing who is in charge and changing what is possible. Replacing a bad manager of a bank is an organizational fix. Deposit insurance, capital requirements, and limited liability are institutional ones. The chapter on the commons is largely a story about the second kind of change.

The rules in use are not the rules in form. Elinor Ostrom drew the distinction that matters most for anyone trying to reform something. Rules-in-form are what the statute, the handbook, or the org chart says. Rules-in-use are what people actually do, and what they expect others to do, when it counts. The two diverge constantly, and the gap is where reforms go to die. A policy that exists on paper and not in behaviour is not a weak institution; it is not an institution at all.

That loop is the reason institutional questions are never settled once. The rules determine which players succeed; the successful players then have both the motive and the means to protect the rules that made them. It also connects directly to path dependence and lock-in: institutions are the most durable artefacts of critical junctures, and the hardest to reopen.

Two problems that look alike and aren't

Institutions solve problems of two kinds, and conflating them produces bad design advice.

A coordination problem has no conflict of interest in it. Everyone prefers that some convention exist, and is indifferent about which — the side of the road you drive on, the shape of an electrical plug, which meridian is zero, whether the meeting is at the top or the bottom of the hour. What is needed is a focal point everyone can converge on, and it can come from almost anywhere: a dominant player, a standard-setting body, a historical accident nobody remembers. Pure coordination problems can be solved by announcement. Standard time is the classic case: American railroads adopted a shared system in 1883, on their own authority, because running trains on dozens of local solar times was killing people in collisions.

A cooperation problem does have conflict in it. Each party does better if the others comply and they don't — paying tax, honouring a contract, not overfishing, not using performance-enhancing drugs, not running a deficit-financed arms race. These cannot be solved by announcement, because announcing changes nothing about anyone's incentives. They require monitoring, sanction, or a change in the payoffs themselves. This is the collective action problem in its various forms, and the multipolar trap when several such problems interact.

Problem Why it fails Institutional fix How the fix fails
Coordination — side of the road, plug shape, standard time Nothing forces a choice among equally good conventions A focal point, a standard, a mandate Two conventions coexist and neither side wants to move first
Cooperation — commons, tax, contracts, honesty Each prefers to defect while others comply Monitoring, graduated sanctions, rule-making by participants Enforcement costs; free-riding on the enforcer; capture of the rule-maker
Commitment — promises about one's own future behaviour Incentives change once others have acted Binding rules, independent bodies, third-party enforcement Rigidity; the binding constraint outlives the problem it solved

Read the last column as carefully as the third. Every institutional fix has its own characteristic decay, and most of the criticism of institutions is really a description of a fix whose failure mode has arrived.

The anatomy: formal rules, informal constraints, enforcement

Every institution has three components, and all three are needed for the thing to exist in practice.

Formal rules — constitutions, statutes, regulations, charters, contracts, standards, written procedures. They are relatively cheap to change, which is why reform efforts gravitate to them and why they are usually the least of the story.

Informal constraints — norms, customs, conventions, professional ethics, taboos, and the ordinary expectations people hold about each other. They change slowly, they are largely invisible to outsiders, and they do most of the work. A society where people queue has no need of queue police.

Enforcement — the machinery that makes non-compliance costly. Ostrom's useful taxonomy distinguishes three kinds: first-party enforcement (you sanction yourself — conscience, habit), second-party enforcement (the party you wronged retaliates), and third-party enforcement (an external authority — a court, a regulator, a referee). Enforcement tends to migrate toward third parties as the scale of exchange grows, because it is the only kind that works between strangers.

Enforcement costs are the design constraint that separates elegant institutions from expensive ones. A rule that requires constant policing is a rule with a permanent budget and a permanent constituency of evaders. A self-enforcing rule — one where compliance is in each party's own interest given what everyone else is doing — costs almost nothing to run and is nearly impossible to abolish. Most institutional design is a search for that property.

Why they matter: four functions

They lower transaction costs, which is what makes impersonal exchange possible. North's central claim is that institutions exist to reduce uncertainty, and that their payoff shows up as the cost of making deals with people you will never see again. In a small village, everyone knows everyone, reputation does the policing, and no courts are needed. Beyond that scale, trade requires a stranger to hand over goods or money on a promise — and without third-party enforcement, credit, insurance, and modern firms are impossible. This is why the interesting institutional history is the history of impersonal trust, not personal trust.

They solve commitment problems. The trouble with a promise about your own future behaviour is that the incentives that made it change later. Governments want to borrow cheaply but cannot promise not to inflate the debt away; a ruler who binds himself is more creditworthy than one who rules by decree. North and Weingast's account of the Glorious Revolution makes exactly this move: constitutional limits on the Crown, and courts that creditors could rely on, were what allowed England to borrow at rates previously unavailable. The same structure appears at every scale — independent central banks, constitutional entrenchment, a firm's charter, a prenuptial agreement, a pre-registered trial protocol. Each is a device for making a future promise credible by making it expensive to break.

They aggregate, certify, and transmit information. Weights and measures, accounting standards, credit bureaus, audited financial statements, professional licensing, scientific publication conventions. Institutions of this kind exist because verifying a claim directly is expensive, so we certify it once and trust the certificate. This is the function most vulnerable to the failure discussed under Goodhart's Law: the moment the certificate becomes the thing people compete for, it stops certifying. The replication crisis is what happens when an information institution's incentive structure drifts away from its informational purpose.

They pool risk and govern shared resources. Insurance, banking systems, and commons governance all rest on rules that make it safe for individuals to accept risk that would ruin them alone, or to restrain themselves on the understanding that others will too. The commons chapter sets out Ostrom's design principles for the second case; the point to carry here is that these arrangements are neither a market nor a state but a third kind of institution, built and enforced by the participants themselves.

Worked example: enforcement without a state

In the eleventh century, Mediterranean trade was conducted by merchants who could not rely on any authority to enforce contracts across borders. Avner Greif's study of two trading communities is the standard illustration of how institutions substitute for a state.

The Maghribi traders, a coalition of Jewish merchants operating from North Africa, enforced honesty through a closed network: cheat one member and the entire coalition boycotts you. Enforcement is second-party and reputation-based, it works well, and it is cheap. Its ceiling is scale: the coalition has to stay small enough that every member's behaviour is observable to the others, and entry has to be controlled to keep information reliable.

The Genoese took a different route. They developed formal instruments — notarised contracts, the sea loan, bilateral partnerships — that let an individual merchant deal with a stranger outside the community, backed ultimately by law rather than by the group. It was more expensive per transaction, slower, and less personal. It also scaled without limit.

The lesson is not that one was better. It is that the enforcement technology available determines the reach of the market. A coalition-based institution optimises for trust inside a boundary and forecloses exchange outside it; a legal institution trades intimacy for reach. Greif's phrase for the underlying problem — that whoever moves second in an exchange has an incentive to renege — is the reason enforcement is not a detail of economic life but its precondition.

Worked example: the two Koreas, and how much the natural experiment really shows

The strongest popular evidence for "institutions matter" is the Korean peninsula. One people, one language, one culture, a shared history, and a border drawn in 1945. Within two generations the two halves diverged by an order of magnitude in income and in almost every measure of welfare. Same geography in the broad sense, same starting population, radically different rules.

The example is genuinely informative, and it is also a single observation with confounds that a careful reader should name. The North's initial endowment was not worse — it inherited most of the peninsula's industry and mineral wealth. But the two states also fought a catastrophic war, received different amounts of foreign patronage, and adopted different political systems along with different economic ones, so "institutions" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The claim survives, but as the direction of a large effect rather than as a measured coefficient. Acemoglu and Robinson generalise it into a distinction between inclusive institutions — broad, secure property rights, open entry, constraints on power — and extractive ones that channel resources to a narrow group. The correlation is strong and replicated across many settings; the causal identification is contested, as it should be, by people doing the kind of historical work that cannot run a controlled trial. The right response is the one this book keeps recommending: hold the claim with a stated number and a stated settlement condition, not as a slogan.

Institutions are not automatically good

Emergence is a claim about origin, not merit, and the same is true of institutions. Three failure modes deserve naming.

Extraction and capture. Institutions distribute; whoever the distribution favours will defend it, and they usually have the means. Regulatory bodies staffed by the industry they oversee, professional guilds that restrict entry, zoning rules that preserve the incumbents' asset values — all are institutions solving a real coordination problem for someone. This is a multipolar trap's gentler cousin: nothing is illegal, everyone is behaving rationally, and the outcome is a rule that exists to prevent competition.

Decay under success. Institutions that work attract resources and power, and the people who hold that power are not the people the institution was built to serve.

Survival without merit. Because institutions are non-perishable, age is evidence of durability rather than of quality — the Lindy effect at work, with the caveat that comes with it. Bloodletting survived two millennia. Durable arrangements persist because switching is expensive, not because they are good, which is precisely the path-dependence story: ask what you would choose from scratch, with switching costs erased, and see whether the answer is the same.

One more caution, because the is/ought gap is easy to cross here. "This institution evolved" describes an origin. "This institution is legitimate" is a value claim, and it needs a bridge premise that no amount of institutional history supplies. The argument that a working arrangement deserves deference because people have organised their lives around it is a real argument — it is about stability and the cost of disruption — but it is a normative one, and it should be stated as such.

How institutions change, and why transplants fail

Institutional change is not gradual. It looks like punctuated equilibrium: decades of stability, then a shock — war, depression, pandemic, a technological discontinuity — opens a critical juncture in which alternatives that were previously unthinkable become available, and a burst of change resets the path for a generation. The window closes quickly, and which option wins is often decided by something small.

The most common mistake in institutional reform is the transplant: importing the formal rules of a successful institution into a context that lacks the informal constraints, the organizations, and the enforcement capacity that made them work. A constitution, a central bank charter, or a corporate governance code copied from elsewhere is a set of rules-in-form. Without the corresponding rules-in-use — the habits of compliance, the professional norms, the courts that people actually believe in — it produces paperwork rather than behaviour. This is where the legibility problem bites from the other side: the reformer can only see and copy what is legible, which is exactly the layer that mattered least.

Ostrom's answer was not a better blueprint but a different ambition: polycentric governance, in which many overlapping, nested institutions make and enforce rules at the scale where the knowledge and the stakes actually live. Not one global commons regime, but a lattice of them — which is the same argument as designing rules rather than outcomes, applied to the rules themselves.

A practical audit

Four questions do most of the work when you encounter any institution, and they take about five minutes.

  1. Whose behaviour does this rule actually govern, and what do those people actually do? Not what the handbook says. Watch.
  2. Who enforces it, at what cost, and who bears that cost? If the answer is "everyone, a little, invisibly" the rule is self-enforcing and durable. If it is "a department, a lot, out of someone else's budget," expect evasion and eventual decay.
  3. Who gains and who pays, and are they the same people? Institutions that concentrate benefits and diffuse costs are stable and quietly corrosive. The reverse are fragile and often worth defending.
  4. What would tell you it is failing? Name the observation in advance. Compliance rates, enforcement costs, and the gap between the reported metric and a directly observed sample are the three that surface decay earliest.

Two design heuristics follow. Prefer rules over outcomes, because rules are what you can actually specify and revise, and because outcome targets inside a system get gamed — the lesson of Goodhart's Law and of emergent order alike. And design for the enforcement you will really have, not the enforcement you would like. A rule that expects a level of monitoring nobody will fund is not ambitious; it is decorative.

Where it bites

  • Development and growth — why capital and technology flow to some places and not others, and why "just add markets" is an incomplete recipe.
  • Commons and shared resources — fisheries, aquifers, forests, and the design of the rules that keep them standing.
  • Firms — why a company has a boundary at all, and why internally coordinated teams and externally contracted ones behave differently.
  • Science — the credibility revolution is a repair job on the institutional layer of a hybrid order.
  • Platforms and online governance — moderation, identity, and reputation are institutional problems being solved, badly and quickly, in public.
  • International relations — with no world government, every treaty is an exercise in self-enforcement or third-party enforcement by consent.
  • Your own commitments — contracts, savings rules, and pre-commitment devices are institutions at the scale of one person, and they work for the same reasons.

Terminology

Institution — a stable, shared, enforced expectation about behaviour; a rule together with the machinery that makes it hold.

Rules-in-form vs rules-in-use (Ostrom) — what the written rule says versus what participants actually do and expect; only the second constitutes a working institution.

Organization (North) — a group of individuals bound by a common purpose; the players, as distinct from the rules they play by.

Coordination problem — a situation with no conflict of interest, in which everyone prefers that some convention exist and is roughly indifferent about which; solved by a focal point.

Cooperation problem — a situation in which each party prefers that others comply while they defect; requires monitoring, sanction, or altered payoffs.

Commitment problem — the difficulty of making a credible promise about one's own future behaviour when future incentives will differ.

Credible commitment — an arrangement that makes reneging costly enough to be believed; the function served by constitutions, independent bodies, and third-party enforcement.

Transaction cost — the cost of arranging, monitoring, and enforcing an exchange, over and above the price of the thing exchanged.

Impersonal exchange — trade between parties with no personal knowledge of one another; impossible without third-party enforcement.

First-, second-, and third-party enforcement — sanctioning by oneself (conscience, habit), by the wronged party (retaliation, boycott), or by an external authority (courts, regulators, referees).

Self-enforcing rule — a rule whose compliance is in each participant's interest given what others do; cheap to run and very hard to abolish.

Inclusive vs extractive institutions (Acemoglu and Robinson) — institutions that grant broad, secure rights and open entry versus those that channel resources and power to a narrow group.

Institutional capture — the process by which the parties an institution was built to regulate come to control its rules.

Institutional decay — the drift of an institution toward serving its members rather than its purpose, usually while its published metrics continue to look healthy.

Institutional transplant — importing formal rules into a context lacking the informal constraints, organizations, and enforcement capacity that made them work elsewhere.

Institutional complementarity — the dependence of a formal rule's effectiveness on matching norms, organizations, and enforcement structures.

Polycentric governance (Ostrom) — a system of many overlapping, nested institutions that make and enforce rules at the scale where the relevant knowledge and stakes sit.

Critical juncture — a period of institutional fluidity in which previously settled alternatives become live and small events determine which rule locks in.
