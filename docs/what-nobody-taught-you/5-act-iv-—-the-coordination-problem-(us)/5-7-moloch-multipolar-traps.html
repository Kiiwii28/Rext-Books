5-7 Moloch - multipolar traps

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

A multipolar trap is a situation in which everyone involved would prefer a different outcome, everyone involved is doing something entirely reasonable, and the outcome is bad anyway. Its defining feature is the absence of a villain. Nobody has to be greedy, shortsighted, or malicious for the trap to close; the participants can describe the structure perfectly and still be unable to leave it. Once you can see this shape, you find it everywhere — in antibiotic resistance, exam prep, advertising, fisheries, litigation, arms races, and the thirty seconds before every meeting where somebody proposes a new metric.

The anatomy of a trap

Start with the smallest version, the prisoner's dilemma. Two players each choose to cooperate or defect; the payoffs are:

B cooperates B defects
A cooperates A: 3, B: 3 A: 0, B: 5
A defects A: 5, B: 0 A: 1, B: 1

Whatever B does, A does better by defecting — defect against a cooperator and you get 5 instead of 3; defect against a defector and you get 1 instead of 0. The move is dominant. The same reasoning runs for B, so both defect, and the pair lands on (1, 1). Yet both would clearly have preferred (3, 3). The outcome is a Nash equilibrium — nobody can improve by changing strategy alone — and it is simultaneously Pareto-dominated, meaning everyone could be better off at a different outcome.

That gap between "no one can move" and "everyone would prefer to move" is the whole phenomenon. Three ingredients produce it:

  • A competitive margin. Something is being contested — a resource, a rank, a market, a mate, a vote — and effort placed on that margin buys an advantage.
  • An uninternalized externality. The cost of your move lands somewhere other than on you: on other users of a shared resource, on your rivals' customers, on strangers, or on the future. If the cost did land on you, the market would handle it.
  • No ability to contract your way out. The parties either cannot agree, cannot trust each other's promises, or cannot verify compliance.

That last point is where Ronald Coase's insight bites: externalities are fundamentally a bargaining failure. When the affected parties are few, well defined, and able to write and enforce an agreement, they will usually fix the problem themselves — the polluter and the downstream fishermen can strike a deal. When the parties are numerous, anonymous, and unable to check whether anyone is cheating, they cannot, and the trap holds. Every escape route in this chapter works by attacking one of the three ingredients.

From two players to many: why it ratchets

The label multipolar comes from Nick Bostrom's work on the strategic risks of advanced artificial intelligence, where he used it for competitive scenarios in which each agent is driven toward a course of action it would rather avoid, because any agent that unilaterally restrained itself would simply be outcompeted. The AI context is exotic; the structure is ancient.

What the many-player version adds is a ratchet. With two players choosing cooperate or defect, the trap is a one-step collapse. With many players and a continuous variable — how much to spend, how many fish to take, how hard to compete — the level climbs and never comes back down, because at every point along the climb the locally best move is to add a little more.

Two features make the ratchet worse than the collapse. First, it is invisible: each individual step is small, defensible, and looks like a normal decision. Second, it is sticky: when pressure eases, nobody steps back down, because stepping down first is exactly the move that was punished on the way up. This is why arms races, ad budgets, and regulatory standards so rarely return to earlier levels even in peacetime, and it is the reason traps are a close relative of path dependence — the level you are at is a residue of history rather than a response to current conditions.

Five shapes it takes

Almost every trap you meet is a variation on one of these. The differences matter, because they determine which escape is available.

Shape What is being contested The move Concentrated benefit Diffuse cost
Arms race Relative capability Escalate Security, deterrence, market share Everyone's budget
Commons depletion A shared rivalrous resource Take more, sooner Yield, catch, grazing Depletion for all users
Race to the bottom Mobile investment or customers Lower standards first Jobs, capital, custom Standards across the jurisdiction
War of attrition / dollar auction A single prize, all-or-nothing Continue just a little longer The prize, and dodging a loss Both parties' escalating spend
Positional competition Rank, relative to others Consume to keep up Status, admission, mates Everyone's time and money, with no aggregate gain

The last one deserves a note, because it is the most common and the least recognized. A positional good is one you value only by comparison — a place at the top school, a house in the good catchment area, a title. Competition for positional goods is a pure ratchet: the total stock of status never increases, so the escalation burns resources without producing anything anyone actually wanted. This is the mechanism behind the education arms race, credential inflation, and the peculiar sensation of running very fast to stay in place. (The best-known trap of all, the depletion of a shared resource, is the commons; it earns its own treatment next.)

Worked example: the dollar auction

The cleanest demonstration available is Martin Shubik's 1971 dollar auction, and it fits in a paragraph.

An auctioneer offers a $1 bill. Bidding starts at 5 cents and rises in 5-cent increments. The twist: both the highest and the second-highest bidders pay, but only the highest receives the dollar.

The trap springs at around 55 cents. The two leading bidders now face a choice that is locally rational and globally absurd: withdraw and lose 50 cents outright, or bid 60 and pay 60 cents for a dollar — which is still, at that instant, a good deal. Both bid. The escalation continues until bids exceed $1, at which point every further bid increases a certain loss in order to avert a larger one. Pairs of rational adults have bid many dollars for a single dollar bill in classrooms, repeatedly, while fully understanding the structure.

Three features are worth extracting:

  • The sunk cost is genuine, not imagined. Past bids are unrecoverable, but they change the payoff of quitting now, because quitting converts them from "spent in pursuit of a prize" into "lost." The trap uses a real accounting change, not a psychological error alone.
  • Asymmetry of loss, not hope of gain. Past a certain point, both players are choosing between a small loss and a larger one. Nobody is being greedy; both are minimizing damage.
  • There is no unilateral exit. Either bidder could have declined at the start. Neither can stop the other's escalation, and stopping alone guarantees the loss.

Real instances share the anatomy: litigation that both sides would settle if they could coordinate, price wars that destroy an industry's margins, bidding wars for acquisitions, campaign spending, R&D races. In each case, the participants are not confused. They are stuck.

Worked example: antibiotic resistance

The most consequential contemporary example runs on a slow clock, which is why it has been allowed to get this far.

Antibiotic resistance is a commons problem with a delay. The resource — the continued effectiveness of a class of drugs — is shared by everyone on the planet and belongs to no one. The move is to use antibiotics generously: prescribe them when a patient pressures you, use them prophylactically in livestock, use broad-spectrum ones because a culture takes two days.

Trace the incentives:

  • The benefit is concentrated and immediate. The patient recovers and stops calling. The farm's animals grow cheaper. The prescriber avoids a difficult conversation and a bad review score.
  • The cost is diffuse and delayed. Every unnecessary prescription contributes a tiny increment to resistance, which lands on strangers, decades in the future, through a mechanism no individual can see.
  • Unilateral restraint is actively punished. A doctor who withholds antibiotics today pays the full cost — patient dissatisfaction, and the occasional missed infection — while the benefit to the global commons is imperceptible. The structure pays for overuse and fines restraint.

Run the unilateral-restraint test on this and you get a clear verdict: materially punished, with no compensating reward. That is a structural trap, and it is why individual virtue alone has never been a sufficient fix. The available escapes work on each ingredient in turn — stewardship programs and prescribing audits make behaviour visible (reducing transaction costs), a regulatory ban on growth-promotion use supplies an enforcer, rapid diagnostics that distinguish bacterial from viral infection change the arena, professional norms change the payoffs, and "subscription" payment models that decouple drug company revenue from volume attack the incentive at its source.

Why "Moloch"

The name most often attached to this family of outcomes comes from outside economics. Moloch was a Canaanite deity associated with child sacrifice, mentioned in Leviticus and given a memorable role by Milton in Paradise Lost as the fiercest of the fallen angels. Allen Ginsberg's Howl (1956) turned the name into a one-word indictment of industrial civilisation — "Moloch whose mind is pure machinery!" In 2014, Scott Alexander's essay "Meditations on Moloch" stitched the older literary image to the game-theoretic structure, and the name stuck in a certain corner of the internet.

The name earns its place for one reason: it names an outcome without naming a perpetrator. Ordinary causal language is built for agents — someone did this, someone is to blame — and traps break that language. "Who is responsible for credential inflation?" has no answer, and the search for one produces conspiracy theories and scapegoats. "Moloch" is a shorthand that stops that search and points at the structure instead.

But the metaphor carries a real hazard, and it is worth stating plainly: Moloch is not an agent. There is no entity with preferences pursuing child sacrifice. The moment the word is used as though it were a force, an explanation, or an excuse, it stops being analysis and becomes a way of saying "the system made me do it." The structure is always just a payoff matrix, an externality, and a set of agents. If you cannot point to those three things, you have not identified a trap; you have invoked a mood.

This is also the sharpest demonstration of a point made earlier in this act: emergent order is not salvation. A multipolar trap is a spontaneous order — nobody designed it, nobody wants it, and it is stable. Emergence is a claim about where a pattern came from, never about whether it is good.

The two mistakes

The concept has a characteristic failure mode in each direction, and they are worth separating because they are often confused.

The first is blame-the-villain thinking. Human causal cognition reaches for an agent, so we look for the bad actor behind a structural outcome — the greedy executive, the lazy regulator, the feckless consumer. Sometimes there is one. Usually the villain is a role someone occupies and would be replaced in within a month.

The second is subtler and, in practice, more dangerous: Moloch laundering. Because structural coercion is a real and respected phenomenon, claiming it is a cheap way to justify what you intended to do anyway. "We have to cut quality, or competitors will." "We can't be the only ones paying a living wage." "There is no alternative." Some of these claims are true. Many are not, and the tell is always the same.

The test is not rhetorical. Ask: name the competitor who would take your customers, the mechanism by which you'd lose, and roughly how much. If that answer exists, you have a trap and should be designing an escape. If it dissolves into vagueness under questioning, you have a preference dressed as a constraint — which is the same move as smuggling a value premise in as a fact.

A genuine trap, moreover, is falsifiable in the ordinary way. It predicts that players who restrain while others continue will lose ground measurably. Look for cases where someone actually did restrain unilaterally and check what happened. That is the difference between a structural claim and an alibi.

Is it always bad?

No — and the distinction matters, because a blanket suspicion of competition is its own error. The same mechanism that produces Moloch produces most of the world's progress, and the difference lies entirely in the payoff structure.

  • Productive competition escalates toward something whose benefits spill over: cheaper solar panels, better vaccines, faster chips, more accurate forecasts. The race is real, the losers lose, and the output is a shared gain. This is the "invisible hand" case — Adam Smith's mechanism producing a public benefit — and it is the same mechanism as a multipolar trap. Nothing about the market changed; the topology of who gains and who pays did.
  • Positional competition escalates toward nothing. The resource is fixed, so effort is consumed without raising the total. This is the pure trap, and the diagnostic is simple: ask whether the escalation raises the aggregate or only the relative position.

Evolution supplies the same spectrum: the peacock's tail and the Red Queen arms race between predator and prey are genuine traps in the individual-cost sense, and they are also where much of biological complexity came from. Some arms races build the world; some just burn it. The question is never "is this competitive?" but "who captures the benefit, and who absorbs the cost?"

Escapes

Every available exit attacks one of the three ingredients: it lowers the cost of contracting, it adds an enforcer, it changes the payoffs, or it changes the arena so the contested margin stops mattering.

Reduce transaction costs. Traps require parties who cannot agree or cannot verify. Fewer players, well-defined rights, repeat interaction, and visibility all attack that. Repeat play is the single strongest lever: when the game is repeated and behaviour is observable, reputation does work that contracts cannot, which is the finding behind Robert Axelrod's tournaments on the iterated prisoner's dilemma — reciprocity succeeds, but only when the "shadow of the future" is long enough and defection is detectable. Making behaviour visible to the group is often all that is needed.

Add an enforcer. Hobbes's answer, and the right one when parties are too numerous to bargain and detection is cheap: an authority that changes the payoffs by fiat. Treaties like the 1987 Montreal Protocol on ozone-depleting substances, bans on growth-promotion antibiotics in livestock, and standardised charging ports are all examples of a third party internalising an externality that the market could not. The failure modes are equally instructive — the enforcer can be captured by those it regulates, can be too weak to detect violations, or can itself be caught in a race to the bottom with other jurisdictions.

Change the payoffs. The cheapest escape and the hardest to build: norms, taboos, and internalised standards that make the defecting move costly to the person doing it, independent of enforcement. Professional ethics, queue discipline, and the refusal to bargain over certain things all work this way. This is also where religion and culture have historically done their heaviest lifting — not by explaining the trap, but by making one branch of it simply unavailable.

Change the arena. Sometimes the contested margin can be removed rather than managed. Vertical integration internalises the externality by merging the players. A technological substitution can make the contested resource irrelevant — a new pricing mechanism, a substitute input, a design that removes the need for the scarce thing. Exit to a different game is a real strategy, and it is usually the one people forget to consider.

Three cautions about the menu. First, escapes are themselves coordination problems: a cartel is an escape from a price war that must solve monitoring and sanctioning, which are the same problems it was trying to escape. Second, every fix creates new margins, and those margins will be found — the second-order effects chapter applies in full. Third, the level is not the goal. The question is not "how do we reduce advertising spending?" but "what is the structure we want, and who pays for it?" A trap broken by a rule that no one can renegotiate has traded one form of stuckness for another.

A diagnostic drill

Run this on any situation that feels like everyone is losing and no one is at fault:

  1. Name the agents and their moves. Who is choosing what, on what margin?
  2. Run the unilateral test. What exactly happens to one player who restrains while the others continue? If you cannot answer specifically, you have not found a trap.
  3. Find the externality. Whose cost is missing from whose payoff? That gap is the engine.
  4. Check the ratchet. Is the level rising over time, or is it static? A static bad equilibrium is a different problem — an unpleasant settlement rather than an escalation.
  5. Ask why contracting fails. Too many parties? Unverifiable behaviour? No trust? No enforcement? The answer names the escape.
  6. Ask whether it should be escaped. Does escalation buy a shared good, or only a relative position?
  7. Name the settlement. In the style of everything else in this book: state what you expect to change, by when, and what you would see if the trap has actually been broken rather than merely relabelled.

The uncomfortable implication, and the reason this topic sits in the act on coordination, is that most of what looks like moral failure at scale is structural. People are not secretly worse than they appear; the incentives reach into ordinary decency and pull out the outcome nobody chose. That is bad news for anyone hoping to fix the world by exhortation. It is good news for anyone willing to work on the rules — because rules are the one thing in the picture that a small number of people can actually change.

Terminology

Multipolar trap — A situation in which multiple agents each have an incentive to take an action that is individually advantageous and collectively self-defeating, with no agent able to improve the outcome unilaterally.

Moloch — Shorthand for the structure, rather than the perpetrator, behind traps; a metaphor drawn from Ginsberg's Howl and popularised by Scott Alexander. Names an outcome with no author, and is a mood rather than an explanation when invoked without a payoff structure.

Prisoner's dilemma — The minimal two-player trap: a dominant move leading both players to a Pareto-dominated outcome they would both prefer to avoid.

Dominant strategy — A move that yields a better payoff than the alternatives regardless of what others do.

Nash equilibrium — An outcome in which no player can improve by changing strategy alone; it can be collectively terrible.

Pareto-dominated / Pareto-superior — An outcome is Pareto-dominated when some alternative makes at least one party better off and none worse off; the alternative is Pareto-superior.

Rivalrous and excludable — Two properties of a good: whether one person's use reduces what is available to others, and whether non-payers can be kept out. Traps live where a good is rivalrous but not excludable.

Common-pool resource — A rivalrous, non-excludable resource — a fishery, aquifer, grazing range, or the atmosphere — where each user's extraction subtracts from every other user's supply.

Externality — A cost or benefit of an action that falls on someone who did not choose it and is not party to any transaction with the actor. The missing term in the trap's payoff.

Transaction costs — The costs of reaching, monitoring, and enforcing an agreement. High transaction costs among numerous parties are the reason traps cannot be bargained away.

Escalation ratchet — The tendency of a trap's contested level to rise and stay risen, because the first party to step back down loses ground.

Arms race — A trap in which competing parties escalate a capability whose value is largely relative, leaving all of them worse off in absolute terms.

Race to the bottom — A trap in which jurisdictions or firms compete by lowering standards, each step justified by the competitive threat of the others.

War of attrition / dollar auction — A trap of continued escalation over a single all-or-nothing prize, in which the rational calculation shifts from winning a gain to minimising a certain loss. Shubik's dollar auction is the canonical demonstration.

Sunk cost — An unrecoverable prior expenditure; in a trap it changes the relative cost of quitting, which is a real accounting effect and not only a psychological error.

Positional good — A good valued for one's rank relative to others; competition for it consumes resources without raising the total, producing escalation with no aggregate gain.

Shadow of the future — Axelrod's term for the prospect of continued interaction; when it is long and behaviour is observable, reciprocity can sustain cooperation that a one-shot game destroys.

Productive vs. positional competition — The distinction between escalation whose output everyone can use and escalation that only rearranges rank; the same competitive mechanism with opposite aggregate verdicts.

Unilateral-restraint test — The practical diagnostic for distinguishing a structural trap from a rhetorical one: identify specifically what a player would lose by restraining alone. No specific loss, no trap.

Moloch laundering — Invoking structural compulsion to justify a preference that would otherwise need defending; the failure mode of the concept in the hands of someone who already knows what they want to do.

Enforcer (Leviathan) solution — Escaping a trap by introducing a third party with the power to change payoffs through regulation, mandate, or treaty; effective when parties are numerous and detection is cheap, vulnerable to capture and to its own competitive races.

Capture — The condition in which the enforcing body comes to serve the interests of those it regulates, converting a solution into a new form of the problem.
