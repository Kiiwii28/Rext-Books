5-8 Moral luck + veil of ignorance

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Two ideas from very different literatures turn out to be a diagnosis and a prescription. Moral luck is the discovery — by Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, in a 1976 volume of essays — that a great deal of what we praise and blame people for depends on factors they did not control. The veil of ignorance is John Rawls's design response: if position is so often an accident, then rules should be chosen by people who do not yet know which position they will occupy. Read together, they give you one of the few genuinely portable tools in this book: a test for whether a rule you are about to impose is one you would accept from underneath.

Moral luck: the control principle and what breaks it

The control principle is the moral intuition almost everyone holds and almost nobody can consistently apply: you are morally assessable only for what is within your control. It underwrites "ought implies can," the excuse of accident, and the difference between a mistake and a crime. Kant built it into the foundations of ethics, and ordinary moral practice assumes it continuously.

Nagel's argument is that almost nothing survives it. He distinguishes four places luck gets in, and they form a chain from who you are to what you did:

  • Constitutive luck — your temperament, talents, capacity for courage or sympathy. Nobody chose to be born patient or quick-tempered.
  • Circumstantial luck — the situations you happen to face. The same person born in Nuremberg in 1920 and in Buenos Aires in 1920 lived under different moral demands. One had the opportunity to be a hero or a collaborator; the other mostly had the opportunity to be unremarkable.
  • Causal luck — how your actions were caused, which is where this runs into the free-will problem. Nagel sets it aside as the deepest case; it is not needed for the argument to bite.
  • Resultant luck — how things turned out. This is the one that disturbs moral practice most, because it is visible every day.

Worked example. Two drivers leave the same pub over the legal limit. Both are equally reckless, equally impaired, equally fortunate in their cars and reflexes. One gets home. The other, at exactly the same speed and reaction time, meets a child who steps into the road half a second earlier. The first driver has a story; the second has a manslaughter conviction, a lifetime of guilt, and a permanent place in the moral record. Nothing that distinguishes them is something either one controlled. We can feel the unfairness and we still — courtrooms, newspapers, and the drivers themselves — treat them as different people.

Williams's contribution is a second, subtler case. His example is a lorry driver who, through no fault whatever, kills a child who runs out in front of him. He is not morally guilty. But Williams argues he nonetheless owes a special kind of response — agent-regret — precisely because it was his lorry and his hands on the wheel. It is not ordinary regret (which anyone might feel) and not guilt (which would presume fault). It is the first-person residue of having been the vehicle of a catastrophe, and telling the driver "you did nothing wrong" does not dissolve it. Moral luck therefore contaminates not only how others judge you but how you must judge yourself.

The result is what Nagel called an antinomy: two things we cannot give up, which cannot both be true. We cannot abandon the control principle without turning morality into a lottery. We cannot abandon our practices of praise, blame, and punishment without abandoning moral life altogether. Nagel's honest conclusion is that this is not a puzzle with a clever solution; it is a structural feature of morality that we mostly paper over.

What the gap is good for

You cannot dissolve moral luck, but you can act on the knowledge that it exists, and the payoff is mostly institutional.

Separate blame from liability. Blame is retrospective: it asks what you deserve, and it is contaminated by luck. Liability is forward-looking: it asks who should bear a cost, and it is a design question. A hospital can require a surgeon whose patient died to undergo review — because review reduces future deaths — while declining to assert that she is a worse person than a colleague whose identical patient survived. Consequence and condemnation are separate judgements that habit fuses.

Assess process where you can, outcome where you must. Outcome-based judgement is the source of resultant luck and of survivorship bias; purely process-based judgement loses the discipline that makes care matter, and invites the failure mode where the metric replaces the goal. Most good systems mix: judge the process contemporaneously, judge the outcome only across many cases where the noise averages out.

Expect the just-world reflex. People have a strong tendency to infer desert from outcome — the successful must have deserved it, the unlucky must have done something. This is a comfort mechanism that makes the world feel ordered, and it is wrong in exactly the way survivorship bias is wrong. It is also the main reason the veil of ignorance feels counterintuitive to people who are well placed: if your position is earned, there is no reason to choose rules without knowing it.

The veil of ignorance

Rawls's original position is a thought experiment about how to choose the basic rules of a society. Parties in it are rational, mutually disinterested, and choosing principles to govern their shared institutions — but they choose behind a veil of ignorance. They do not know their class, their natural talents, their family, their conception of the good, or which generation they belong to. They know general facts about economics and psychology; they do not know who they are.

It is worth being precise about what this device is. It is not a state of nature, not a bargaining session, and not a claim about how societies began. It is a device of representation: a way of modelling the requirement that a rule not be tailored to advantage whoever happens to be writing it. Rawls's phrase for what it screens out is that the distribution of benefits should not be settled by factors "arbitrary from a moral point of view."

The mechanism is straightforward. If you do not know whether you will be the landlord or the tenant, the healthy or the ill, the majority or the minority, then self-interest no longer tells you to write a rule for the strong. Rawls argued that parties so situated would choose two principles: equal basic liberties for all, and social and economic inequalities permitted only when they also improve the position of the worst off — the difference principle.

That conclusion is contested, and one objection is worth knowing because it defines the tool's sensitivity. John Harsanyi argued that if the parties are genuinely ignorant, they should assign equal probability to each position and maximize average utility — a straightforward expected-value calculation. Rawls's parties instead reason as if the worst outcome is the one that matters, which is maximin. The two produce different societies. Which is right depends on what you assume about the choosers' attitude to risk — which means the veil does not generate answers by itself. It relocates disagreement from "what should we do?" to "what would reasonable people behind the veil agree to?" — a better question, but still a question.

Using it as a tool rather than a theory

Most people will never settle the Harsanyi–Rawls dispute, and do not need to. The practical value of the veil is that it is a fast consistency check on any rule you are about to impose, and it can be run in a few minutes.

Step three is the one people skip and the one that does the work. You cannot run the test without naming the affected population and identifying the worst position in it — which is why the veil is a useful complement to baselines and base rates: it forces you to look at the tail rather than the typical case.

Worked example: a firm's layoff rule. A company needs to cut 10% of costs. Option A: lay off the 10% with the weakest performance reviews. Option B: a 10% pay cut across the board, no layoffs. Option C: reduce the dividend and the executive bonus pool first, then a smaller pay cut.

Run the veil. Population: employees, shareholders, customers. Positions: top performer with a mortgage, middling performer with a sick parent, recent hire, long-tenured specialist whose role has been automated, shareholder, customer. Worst positions: the worker laid off in a weak local job market, and the worker whose pay falls below what the household needs. Behind the veil, not knowing which you are, Option A looks like a lottery over ruin; Option C concentrates the pain where it is most survivable. Notice that the tool has not told you that layoffs are wrong. It has told you that if you would not accept a rule knowing you might be the one it destroys, you owe an argument for why not — an actual argument, not the observation that it is efficient.

Worked example: drought pricing. A water utility must cut consumption by 20%. Option A: a flat restriction on all households. Option B: steeply rising per-litre prices. Option C: a free allocation per person, with trading above it. Behind the veil, you do not know whether you are a large household, a small one, a landlord passing costs to tenants, or a business whose competitors are in the next jurisdiction. Option B's burden falls hardest on people for whom water is a large share of the budget; Option C preserves the signal while capping the damage; Option A ignores that the same litre means different things to different households. Again the tool produces a critique rather than a verdict — and the critique is where the useful argument starts.

Where it fails

Three honest limits.

You can smuggle yourself back in. Whoever specifies the parties — their risk aversion, their preferences, whether they care about future generations — determines much of the answer. A person who wants low taxes can populate the original position with parties who happen to dislike redistribution. The veil is a consistency check, not an oracle; it detects rules that advantaged their author, and it cannot manufacture agreement where values genuinely differ.

The boundary problem is unresolved. Who gets a seat behind the veil? Rawls's own treatment of future generations is notoriously awkward, and non-human interests and outsiders who never get to negotiate fit poorly. Every real application involves drawing a line that some affected party does not get to help draw.

It does not generate compliance. Knowing what a fair rule is does not make anyone follow it, which is why moral luck and the veil both sit inside an act about coordination rather than an act about knowledge. Fairness arguments operate on people who are already persuaded; the rest is institutions, incentives, and the multipolar traps covered elsewhere in this act.

Where the two ideas meet

Moral luck is the empirical claim; the veil is the procedural response. If a large share of your position, your character, and your outcomes is not your doing, then a rule chosen by people who know they hold the good position will encode an accident as a desert.

The link is not merely thematic. It explains why the veil is politically unstable: it asks people to reason as though their advantages were contingent, and the just-world reflex — reinforced by every survivorship-biased success story they have ever read — tells them the opposite. It also explains what the veil is for. It is not a machine for producing just institutions. It is a machine for catching the moment when you were about to write your own position into a rule and call it neutral. That is a smaller claim than Rawls's, and it is one you can use this afternoon.

Where it bites

  • Severance and restructuring — choosing a layoff rule behind the veil rather than defending the one that protects you.
  • Sentencing and liability — the drunk driver example, generalized: outcome-based penalties when outcomes are noisy.
  • Insurance and credit — pricing that is actuarially accurate and veil-unacceptable, and the reverse.
  • Algorithmic systems — the appeal procedure matters more than the model, because you do not know whether you will be the one who needs it.
  • Disaster relief and pandemic policy — rules written before you know which neighbourhood you live in.
  • Admissions, funding, and promotion — where the assessor's own path is the unexamined template.
  • Family and team rules — the same test at small scale, where it is cheapest to run and most often skipped.

Running the drill

  1. State the rule as a rule, not as a decision.
  2. Name everyone affected, including people who cannot speak in the decision.
  3. List the positions and identify the worst one.
  4. Ask whether you would accept the rule without knowing which position is yours.
  5. If not, name what would have to change — a floor, a compensation, a different rule — and then ask what your actual objection was.

Step five is where the honesty lives. Often the answer is that you would accept the rule, and you simply prefer your current position to be protected. That is a legitimate thing to want, but it is a value claim, not a fairness claim, and it belongs on the table as one.

Terminology

Moral luck — The phenomenon that a person's moral record depends substantially on factors beyond their control; Nagel's and Williams's term for the tension this creates with the control principle.

Control principle — The intuition that agents are morally assessable only for what they could control; the assumption moral luck undermines.

Constitutive luck — Luck in who one is: temperament, capacities, and dispositions that shape action without being chosen.

Circumstantial luck — Luck in the situations and moral demands one happens to face; the reason the same person can have radically different moral records in different times and places.

Causal luck — Luck in how one's actions were caused; the free-will dimension of the problem.

Resultant luck — Luck in how an action turned out; the source of the drunk-driver asymmetry and of outcome-based blame generally.

Agent-regret (Williams) — The special first-person response of someone who, though blameless, was the vehicle of a harm; not guilt, and not dissolved by being told one is blameless.

Antinomy (of moral luck) — The unresolvable conflict between the control principle and ordinary moral practice; Nagel's claim is that it has no clean solution.

Just-world tendency — The inclination to infer desert from outcome, so that the successful and the unlucky are assumed to have earned their positions; the main psychological obstacle to veil reasoning.

Original position (Rawls) — The hypothetical choice situation in which parties select principles of justice for their society.

Veil of ignorance — The restriction in the original position that parties do not know their own class, talents, family, or conception of the good; a device for preventing rules from being tailored to their author's position.

Device of representation — Rawls's description of the original position: not a historical or psychological claim, but a way of modelling impartiality.

Difference principle — Rawls's claim that inequalities are just only if they also improve the position of the worst-off group.

Maximin — The decision rule of choosing the option whose worst outcome is least bad; Rawls's characterization of choice behind the veil.

Harsanyi's objection — The argument that genuinely ignorant parties should assign equal probabilities across positions and maximize average utility, yielding a different (broadly utilitarian) conclusion than Rawls's maximin.

Arbitrary from a moral point of view — Rawls's phrase for the natural and social contingencies — birth, talent, family — that should not determine distributive outcomes.

Smuggling — The failure mode in which the person specifying the parties behind the veil encodes their own preferences into the parties' preferences, making the device yield the answer they already wanted.
