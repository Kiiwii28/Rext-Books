5-1 Collective action problems

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Collective action problems are the class of situations in which each person does something defensible and the group gets an outcome nobody wanted. The defining feature is not conflict — it is misalignment. Everyone would prefer the cooperative result; everyone behaves reasonably given what they expect others to do; and the result is worse than any of them intended. This is why the standard remedies fail. Exhortation, appeals to shared values, and reminders that "we're all in this together" do not move the structure that produced the behaviour. They address the character of people who are already reasoning correctly under the wrong payoffs.

The practical consequence, and the thesis of this subsection: treat a collective action problem as a defect in a payoff structure, not a defect in a person. Then ask which part of the structure you can reach.

The anatomy

Two ingredients produce the classic case.

First, a good whose benefits cannot be confined to the people who pay for it — non-excludable, and often non-rival as well. Clean air, a stable climate, a defended border, a tidy shared kitchen, an accurate Wikipedia page, a company's reputation, herd immunity.

Second, a divergence between private and social returns: the act of contributing costs the contributor more than it returns to them personally, while returning more to the group as a whole than it costs. That second clause is the one people miss. Collective action problems are not cases where contribution is collectively pointless. They are cases where it is collectively worthwhile and individually unrewarding.

Take two neighbours and a shared public good. A contribution costs 3 and generates 5 of value, split evenly between them.

B contributes B free-rides
A contributes A: +2, B: +2 A: −0.5, B: +2.5
A free-rides A: +2.5, B: −0.5 A: 0, B: 0

Read the diagonal. Mutual contribution gives each +2; mutual free-riding gives each 0. Both would rather be on the diagonal than off it. But free-riding is dominant — better if the other contributes (+2.5 > +2) and better if they don't (0 > −0.5) — so the only stable outcome is the one nobody prefers.

Now look at the returns. An act of contribution returns 5 of value to the pair for a cost of 3: a social return of +2. It returns 2.5 to the contributor for a cost of 3: a private return of −0.5. Every feature of this situation follows from that gap, and so does every remedy: change who pays, change who receives, or change how many times they will meet again.

The 1/N problem

Generalise to N participants sharing the benefit. The private return to contributing becomes 5/N − 3, while the social return stays at +2. At N = 2 the contributor is only slightly underwater. At N = 10 the contributor loses 2.5 to deliver 2.0 of social value. At N = 1,000 contributing is simply irrational.

This is Mancur Olson's central insight in The Logic of Collective Action (1965), and it explains why the difficulty scales with group size in a way intuition does not expect. Larger groups are not just harder to organise; the arithmetic of individual incentive gets worse, and simultaneously the group loses the two things that discipline free-riders: the ability to notice who contributed, and the ability to respond.

Olson's vocabulary is worth keeping:

  • Latent group — a large group with a shared interest and no organisation, because no individual has enough at stake to act alone.
  • Privileged group — a group small enough, or with a member stakes-large enough, that someone will simply provide the good themselves. A firm with 80% market share lobbies for the industry; a small firm free-rides.
  • Exploitation of the great by the small — the resulting pattern, in which the concentrated interest pays and the diffuse interest benefits.

Notice what this means for remedies. Where a privileged actor exists, the good may be provided without any cooperation at all — just unequally paid for. Where no such actor exists, the group typically needs something other than the collective benefit to motivate action: a selective incentive that is excludable, received only by contributors or withheld from defectors. Union representation is a public good to a workplace; the union's private benefits — legal cover, training, insurance, social standing — are what make membership individually rational. That is not corruption of the ideal; it is the standard engineering solution to a structural problem.

The loop that makes it worse

Free-riding is self-reinforcing. Each defection is evidence for the next one's pessimism.

This loop explains the characteristic phenomenology: long periods of apparent equilibrium and tolerance, then a rapid collapse once defection becomes visible enough to justify itself, and — the other direction — a critical mass past which contribution becomes the safe bet and the loop runs in reverse. Public goods provision is often lumpy in exactly this way: a small shift in visible participation can flip the whole system from "nobody bothers" to "everyone does."

Coordination is not cooperation

The single most costly error in this territory is treating all of these situations as one kind of problem. They are at least four.

Structure The risk Typical fix
Pure coordination — everyone wants the same thing, only the choice is arbitrary (which side of the road, which meeting time, which standard) Nothing happens, or two incompatible conventions appear A focal point: a standard, a default, an authority's announcement
Assurance / stag hunt — all prefer mutual cooperation, but cooperating alone is worse than defecting Nobody wants to be the only one who shows up A credible signal that others are joining: pledges, public commitments, visible early adopters
Prisoner's dilemma — mutual cooperation is best collectively, defection dominates individually Cooperation unravels as each anticipates the other's defection Enforcement, repetition, reputation, changed payoffs
Volunteer's dilemma / chicken — somebody must act, nobody wants to be the one (a lumpy public good like a streetlight or a rescue) Action arrives late, or lands on the wrong volunteer Rotating burdens, assigning responsibility, a privileged actor

The distinctions are not academic. Pure coordination problems involve no temptation to defect at all — the entire difficulty is picking one of several equally good options — so coercion applied to them wastes enforcement capacity and destroys legitimacy for nothing. Prisoner's dilemmas are the reverse: information, exhortation, and goodwill change nothing, because the defector is already behaving optimally. And assurance problems are the ones where publishing a list of who has already pledged does more than any rule could, which is why pledge-and-review architectures recur in international agreements.

A quick diagnostic, run before choosing a remedy:

What makes cooperation possible

Four conditions do most of the work, and their absence predicts failure better than any measure of culture or goodwill.

Repetition. One-shot games reward defection; repeated games reward reputation. The shadow of the future — the expectation that you will meet these people again — is what makes reciprocal strategies viable. Axelrod's tournaments found that tit-for-tat, which cooperates first and thereafter mirrors, was robust precisely because it was nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear — and because everyone could identify and remember one another.

Identifiability and observability. You cannot reciprocate against an anonymous crowd, and you cannot sanction someone whose contribution you cannot see.

Group size and boundary. Smaller, bounded groups sustain cooperation with informal mechanisms alone; larger ones need formal ones. This is the recurring argument for nesting problems at the smallest scale that contains them.

A monitor with an incentive to monitor. Which runs straight into the second-order problem: watching for free-riders is itself a public good, so who watches the watchers? Ostrom's answer — that users monitor each other under locally made, graduated rules — is treated in the next subsection. The general point stands: enforcement is not an exception to collective action theory; it is a collective action problem in its own right, and any solution must survive that recursion.

A menu of levers

All remedies work by altering the structure, not the sentiment.

Lever How it works Example
Selective incentives Attach excludable rewards to contribution or costs to defection Union benefits; loyalty programmes; access to a shared resource conditional on upkeep
Change excludability Make the good you are providing privately capturable Fences, meters, licences, paywalls — technology often does this cheaply
Restructure scale and boundaries Shrink N so the private return rises and observation becomes possible Subsidiarity; neighbourhood-level rather than national-level provision
Lengthen the shadow of the future Make the interaction repeated, remembered, and reciprocal Long-term contracts, relationships, reputational systems
Add monitoring and graduated sanctions Detect and respond proportionally, starting gently Community rules; audits; standards bodies
Shift to a privileged actor or an external authority Someone with enough at stake, or with coercive power, internalises the problem The state (Hobbes's answer); a dominant firm; a trustee
Change the game type Convert a dilemma into an assurance or coordination problem Pledges with published accounting; a standard that removes the arbitrary choice
Exit Let people leave rather than fix the group Markets, federalism, switching costs kept low

Norms, laws, and markets are the three great families these levers belong to, and each has a characteristic failure: norms struggle with scale, laws with local knowledge and legitimacy, markets with goods that cannot be priced. The craft lies in matching the family to the structure — not in preferring one.

Misdiagnosis, and how to avoid it

Because the label "collective action problem" carries an implicit verdict — people won't act, so we must make them — it gets applied to situations that are not one. A team that misses a deadline because two members disagree about priorities has a conflict, not a dilemma. A neighbourhood that fails to organise recycling because nobody knows where the bins are has an information problem. A workforce that resists a change it believes is harmful is expressing a value judgment, and no incentive scheme will resolve it.

The diagnosis should be falsifiable, in the sense this book keeps returning to. Name in advance what would tell you that this is not, in fact, a collective action problem: if contribution is individually irrational, withholding should already be common and should increase with group size. If the opposite is happening — if people are contributing voluntarily and freely — then the constraint is elsewhere, and you are about to apply coercion to a coordination problem, which buys you nothing and spends legitimacy you will want later.

The final check is to ask who benefits from the framing. Calling something a collective action problem locates the fault in the participants and suggests that the remedy is their compliance. Sometimes that is generous and correct. Sometimes it is a manager, a ministry, or a majority describing a dispute about values or priorities as a failure of coordination, because a coordination problem can be solved by the person doing the describing.

Where it bites

  • Climate and atmosphere — the textbook case: large N, non-excludable, non-rival, long horizon, no world government, and a costly transition concentrated on identifiable industries.
  • Antibiotic resistance — each prescriber's precaution is a private cost against a diffuse, delayed, shared benefit.
  • Voting — a single vote effectively never decides an election, so the expressive and selective incentives do all the work.
  • Vaccination and herd immunity — individual protection is largely private; the population-level protection is the public good, and its erosion is self-reinforcing.
  • Open source and Wikipedia — sustained by selective incentives, reputation, and identity rather than by the collective benefit, which is precisely why Olson's framework predicts their survival.
  • Team projects and shared offices — the everyday N-person case, resolved by visibility, rotation, and small-team accountability rather than by exhortation.
  • Cartels, unions, and standards bodies — organisations whose entire purpose is to convert a latent group into a coordinated one, and whose members face a perpetual temptation to cheat.

Terminology

Collective action problem — A situation in which individually rational behaviour produces a collectively worse outcome, because the private return to contributing is smaller than the private cost while the social return exceeds the social cost.

Public good — A good that is non-excludable (no one can be kept from benefiting) and non-rival (one person's use does not reduce another's).

Non-excludability — The property that benefits cannot be confined to those who paid for them; the precondition for free-riding.

Free-riding — Benefiting from a good without contributing to its provision, where the structure makes doing so individually rational.

Private return — The net benefit an individual receives from their own contributing act.

Social return — The net benefit to the group as a whole from that same act; the divergence between private and social return is the problem.

1/N problem — The dilution of any individual's share of a collective benefit as group size grows, which makes contribution progressively less rational for each member.

Latent group (Olson) — A large group sharing an interest but unorganised, because no member has enough at stake to act.

Privileged group (Olson) — A group in which one or a few members have enough at stake to provide the good themselves.

Exploitation of the great by the small — The pattern in which a concentrated, high-stakes interest pays for a good that a diffuse mass of small interests enjoys for free.

Selective incentive — A benefit or penalty available only to contributors (or only to defectors), used to make participation individually rational.

Critical mass — The level of visible participation past which contributing becomes the safe option, so provision becomes self-sustaining.

Coordination problem — A situation in which everyone agrees on the desired outcome but must converge on one of several equivalent choices; contains no incentive to defect.

Assurance problem (stag hunt) — A situation in which all prefer mutual cooperation, but cooperating alone is worse than defecting, so each waits for credible evidence that others will join.

Prisoner's dilemma — A payoff structure in which defection is dominant for each player while mutual cooperation would be better for both.

Volunteer's dilemma — A structure in which a lumpy public good requires at least one contributor, but everyone prefers that someone else be that contributor.

Shadow of the future — The expected probability and duration of future interaction, which determines whether reciprocal strategies can sustain cooperation.

Tit-for-tat — A reciprocal strategy that cooperates on the first move and thereafter mirrors the other party's previous move; robust when interactions are repeated and participants identifiable.

Second-order collective action problem — The difficulty that monitoring and sanctioning free-riders is itself a public good, so enforcement requires a solution of the same kind it is meant to provide.

Selective monitoring — Restricting observation to what is needed to detect defection, keeping enforcement costs below the value of the good.

Graduated sanctions — A response that escalates proportionally rather than punishing maximally, preserving the possibility of restored cooperation.
