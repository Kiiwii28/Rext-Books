5-2 Tragedy of the commons + Ostrom's escape

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

The essay that named the structure

In December 1968, Garrett Hardin published a four-page essay in Science that gave a name to a structure everyone had noticed and nobody had solved. Picture a pasture open to all. Each herder asks whether to add one more animal. The benefit of that animal — the sale price — accrues entirely to the herder. The cost — the extra grazing pressure — is shared among all the herders. So the herder adds the animal, and so does everyone else, until the pasture is stripped. "Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all."

Hardin's diagnosis was sharp and his prescription was blunt: either privatise the resource or submit to "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon." For a quarter-century, that binary — market or state, enclosure or regulation — framed nearly every policy debate about shared resources. Which was odd, because it was wrong.

The mechanism, stated precisely

Strip away the cattle and the tragedy is a specific feedback structure, not a moral failing.

Two features do all the work. The resource is rivalrous — my catch leaves less for you — and non-excludable — nobody can cheaply keep others out. Where both hold, every user receives the full benefit of their own extraction and only a fraction (one nth, if there are n users) of the cost. Restraint is individually irrational even when it is collectively obvious. Worse, the depletion itself feeds the race: as the stock falls, the catch per unit of effort drops, which pressures each user to extract faster before rivals get there. That second loop is why collapsed fisheries are usually overcapitalised — too many boats chasing too few fish — right up to the end.

Placing this in the standard typology makes the boundaries clear:

Excludable Non-excludable
Rivalrous Private goods (bread, a parking space) Common-pool resources (fishery, aquifer, pasture, radio spectrum, atmosphere)
Non-rivalrous Club goods (streaming subscription, gated community) Public goods (national defence, basic research, street lighting)

Hardin was not the first to notice. Aristotle observed that "what is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it," and William Forster Lloyd described the scrawny cattle on common land versus the fat cattle on enclosed land in 1833. What Hardin supplied was a name that travelled — and, crucially, the word tragedy, borrowed from Alfred North Whitehead's line about "the solemnity of the remorseless working of things." The remorselessness was baked into the label. It is also the part that turns out to be false.

One piece of context belongs here, because textbooks that teach the parable usually omit it. Hardin's essay was not only about cattle. It was written at the peak of the population-control panic, framed around the "freedom to breed," and its author went on to argue for refusing food aid to poorer nations and against immigration, in an explicitly hereditarian register that has not aged well. The metaphor outlived the agenda, which is fortunate, but the agenda is part of why the metaphor spread so fast.

Open access is not a commons

The single most consequential correction is a definitional one, and it is Ostrom's.

Hardin's herdsmen were not in a commons. They were in open access — a resource with no rules at all, not even rules about who may use it. A commons, properly understood, is a shared resource governed by a defined community through rules the community accepts: who can use it, how much, when, by what method, and what happens if you cheat. The English commons that Hardin's parable invoked were in fact heavily regulated, with manorial courts, stints limiting how many animals each commoner could graze, and seasonal restrictions — a fact historians of enclosure have documented at length, and which matters because the story of wasteful commons was used to justify the enclosures that transferred common land to large landowners.

The distinction is not pedantry. It changes the diagnosis, the prescription, and the range of available outcomes. If the problem is "a commons," the only fixes are the two Hardin named. If the problem is "a resource with no rules," then the fix is rules — and rules can be made by the users themselves. Hardin himself later acknowledged that the essay would have been more accurate if he had called it "the tragedy of the unmanaged commons," which is a polite way of conceding that the interesting question had been excluded by the title.

Ostrom went and looked

Elinor Ostrom did something unusual for a political economist: instead of reasoning from the model, she collected cases. Governing the Commons (1990), the book that eventually won her the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize — the first awarded to a woman in economics — assembled centuries of successful self-governance alongside the failures.

The successful cases included alpine grazing commons in Törbel, Switzerland, governed by written charters dating to 1224; Japanese village commons (iriaichi) with detailed rules on cutting timber and gathering fuel; the huertas of Valencia and Murcia in Spain, where irrigation water has been allocated by a water court for roughly a thousand years; the zanjera irrigation systems of the northern Philippines, built and maintained by the farmers who use them; and the inshore fisheries of Maine, where informal "harbour gangs" enforced territorial boundaries through a graduated system of warnings, cutting of traps, and eventually exclusion.

The cases that failed were just as informative. What distinguished them was rarely the resource or the culture. It was whether the users had defined boundaries, cheap monitoring, escalating sanctions, and genuine authority to make their own rules.

Eight principles, not a recipe

From the comparison, Ostrom drew out a set of design principles — not a blueprint, but a checklist of features that recurrently distinguish long-enduring institutions from short-lived ones.

Principle What it does
1. Clearly defined boundaries Defines both the resource and who is entitled to use it. Without this, no rule can be enforced against anyone in particular.
2. Congruence with local conditions Rules fit the ecology, the technology, and the work; costs borne are proportional to benefits received.
3. Collective-choice arrangements Most users participate in making and changing the rules. Rules made by the governed are cheap to legitimate.
4. Monitoring Monitors are the users themselves or people accountable to them, and they are publicly accountable for their monitoring.
5. Graduated sanctions First offences draw mild, often informal responses; penalties escalate only with repetition. This keeps punishment cheap and preserves cooperation.
6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms Fast, cheap, legitimate ways to resolve disputes — without them, every dispute becomes a reason to defect.
7. Minimal recognition of rights to organise External authorities do not overrule the users' right to devise their own institutions.
8. Nested enterprises For larger systems, governance is layered — local rules inside regional rules inside national law.

The eighth principle is the one most often skipped, and it is the answer to the scale objection. A single pasture can be governed by a village; a river basin cannot. What makes a large system workable is not one big rulebook but polycentric governance — many overlapping, semi-autonomous centres of decision-making, each handling what it can see, nested inside larger ones handling what it cannot. The Ostroms' term, borrowed from Michael Polanyi, is deliberately unglamorous: not a Leviathan and not a market, but a lattice.

The mechanism, inverted

The escape from the tragedy is not altruism. It is the deliberate re-plumbing of the same loops.

Monitoring turns unobservable extraction into observable. Graduated sanctions convert an externality into a private cost — the cheater now pays something, and the amount rises if they persist, while a first mistake is forgiven so that cooperation remains the better strategy. Collective choice makes the whole apparatus legitimate, which is what makes monitoring cheap: people who helped write the rule report violations they would have resented reporting under a rule imposed on them. And cheap conflict resolution keeps disputes from escalating into the very defection the rules exist to prevent.

Notice that nothing here requires the users to be unusually virtuous. It requires them to be identifiable, to interact repeatedly, to have information about each other's behaviour, and to have the authority to act. Those are structural conditions, not character traits — which is exactly why the tragedy is not inevitable.

Where the escape fails

Ostrom's later career was largely spent warning that her principles are diagnostic, not a recipe. "No panaceas" was her refrain. Self-governance strains or fails under recognisable conditions:

  • Scale. Boundaries become impossible to draw when the resource is global and the user group is everyone. The atmosphere has no edges.
  • Heterogeneity and anonymity. Shared norms do the work in small communities; large, mobile, mutually anonymous populations have neither the information nor the repeat interaction.
  • No history of cooperation. Rules require trust to bootstrap, and trust requires a track record. Institutions cannot be dropped onto a context that lacks the ordinary habits that make them work.
  • Long time lags. If the consequence of overuse arrives in fifty years, the sanctioning loop never closes on the person who caused it.
  • External interference. A government that overrides local rules destroys the very authority that made them enforceable — the classic failure mode in state-imposed quota systems.
  • Rapid change. A shift in technology, market price, or climate can outrun rules that were perfectly fitted to the old conditions.

Climate change is the extreme case, and it strains the principles at every point: no boundary, no shared community, no cheap exclusion, no graduated sanction that lands on the violator, and a delay measured in decades. That is not an argument that the framework is useless — it is an argument that the international climate regime needs more of the design principles, not less: nested institutions, monitoring that is genuinely independent, sanctions that are real but proportionate, and dispute mechanisms that work.

Worked example: two Turkish fisheries

Fikret Berkes studied two inshore fisheries on the same Turkish coast, roughly thirty kilometres apart, at the same time, fishing the same species with comparable boats.

Alanya. Roughly a hundred fishers, all of whom knew each other. Over decades they evolved a rotating system: each fisher had a named fishing spot, and the right to each spot rotated on a lottery each day. The rule was public, the assignment was visible, and compliance was observable because everyone could see who was where. Violations were sanctioned in a graduated sequence — first a mild confrontation, then gossip and social pressure, then, for repeat offenders, exclusion from the rotation entirely. The system survived for decades, was enforced without police, and kept both the fish stock and the fishing community intact.

Bodrum. A larger, more heterogeneous fishery, with different gear types competing for the same water and no such arrangement. There were more fishers than the fishery could support, no shared rule about who fished where, and no mechanism for making one. Conflicts escalated into open hostility and violence between groups.

Run the design principles over the two cases and the difference is not mysterious. Alanya had boundaries (a known, limited set of fishers), congruence (rules matched how the fishery actually worked), collective choice (the fishers made the rules), monitoring (everyone could see everything), graduated sanctions (warnings before exclusion), and conflict resolution (the cooperative and its informal authority). Bodrum had none of them. Same resource, same culture, same decade. Different rules.

Why "tragedy" is the wrong word

The label does real damage, in three ways.

It claims necessity. A tragedy, in Whitehead's sense, is remorseless: the outcome is entailed by the structure. But the structure entails ruin only when there are no rules. Ostrom's evidence is that rule-making is a normal human activity, not an heroic exception.

It smuggles in a prescription. "The commons always fails" is an empirical claim. "Therefore this commons should be enclosed or nationalised" is a conclusion containing something the premise did not — a value judgement about efficiency, or about whose interests should win. That is the is/ought gap wearing a lab coat, and it has been used to justify enclosures, privatisations, and coerced resettlement on the strength of a metaphor.

It becomes self-fulfilling. If everyone, including the users themselves, believes that any shared resource is doomed, then defection looks like realism and rule-making looks like naivety. The narrative is a prior, and priors shape what people attempt — which is the same mechanism that makes predictions in complex systems partly self-causing.

Using it

When you meet a shared resource — a municipal budget, a data set, a team's attention, an aquifer, an office kitchen — the useful move is not to choose between markets and states. It is to diagnose:

  • What exactly is the resource, and can its boundary be drawn? If not, self-governance is not available and nesting becomes necessary.
  • Who are the users, and do they know each other? Identifiability is a precondition, not a detail.
  • Can behaviour be observed cheaply? If not, monitoring must be designed — and it must be designed with the ninth question in mind: who watches the monitors?
  • Is there a graduated ladder of sanctions, with a cheap first rung and a real last one? Binary punishment invites escalation and vendettas.
  • Do the users have real authority to change the rules? A rule that can be overridden from outside is not a rule; it is a request.
  • What nests above it? Local governance cannot handle externalities that cross its boundary. The question is always what the smallest unit is that can manage the resource, and what must sit above it to handle the spillovers.

Ostrom's lasting contribution is that she replaced a binary with a design space — and then went out and measured which parts of it actually hold up. The tragedy was never inevitable. It was a description of a configuration of rules, written by someone who had not looked at the configurations that work.

Terminology

Common-pool resource (CPR) — A resource that is rivalrous in consumption but from which users are difficult to exclude; the class to which the "tragedy" is usually applied.

Rivalry — The property that one user's consumption reduces the amount available to others.

Non-excludability — The property that preventing others from using a resource is infeasible or prohibitively costly.

Tragedy of the commons — Hardin's 1968 claim that open shared resources are inevitably depleted by the rational self-interest of their users.

Open access — A resource with no effective rules governing use; Hardin's actual subject, and not the same thing as a commons.

Commons — A shared resource governed by a defined community through rules about who may use it, how, and with what consequences.

Enclosure — The historical conversion of common land into private property, justified in part by the alleged wastefulness of commons; the political use of the tragedy narrative.

Governing the Commons (Ostrom, 1990) — The empirical study of long-enduring self-governed resource institutions that founded the modern commons literature.

Design principles — Ostrom's eight recurrent features of durable commons institutions: boundaries, congruence, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognition of rights to organise, and nested enterprises.

Graduated sanctions — A penalty ladder beginning with mild or informal responses and escalating only with repeated violation; central to keeping cooperation the better strategy.

Polycentric governance — A system of many overlapping, semi-autonomous decision-making centres, none of which is sovereign over the others; the answer to scale.

Nested enterprises — The layering of governance so that local rules operate inside regional and national ones, each handling problems at the scale it can observe.

Social-ecological system (SES) — Ostrom's later framework for systematically describing the interactions between a resource system, its users, and their governance.

No panaceas — Ostrom's warning that neither markets, nor states, nor community management works everywhere; diagnosis must precede prescription.

Commoning — The ongoing activity of making and enforcing rules for a shared resource, as opposed to the static existence of a "commons."

Multipolar trap (Moloch) — A broader category of which the commons tragedy is the depletable-resource case: individually rational conduct producing a collectively ruinous outcome that no participant chose.
