5-9 Survivorship bias

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Every claim about what works is a claim about a sample — and samples are rarely drawn fairly. Survivorship bias is what happens when the cases you can see are only the ones that made it through some gate, while the ones that didn't left no record behind. Nothing in your data is false. The data are accurate, the numbers add up, and the sample is real. The inference is still wrong, because the population you are generalising from is not the population your claim is about.

Why this is a sampling problem, not a weighting problem

Ordinary sampling bias means your sample is unrepresentative but mixed: you got too many young people, too few rural households. If you know the proportions, you can reweight and repair it. Survivorship bias is worse behaved, because the missing cases are missing because of the outcome you are trying to explain. They are not underrepresented; they are absent, and their absence is systematic.

The thing doing the filtering is called the sampling frame — the rule that determines who could have appeared in your data at all. The frame, not the measurement, is where the lie lives. This is why the bias survives so well in careful, numerate people: they audit the arithmetic and never audit the gate.

Wald's planes

The canonical case comes from the Statistical Research Group in New York during the Second World War. Aircraft were returning from missions with bullet holes, and the question was where to add armour. The obvious reading of the damage reports was: armour the places that get hit most.

Abraham Wald's contribution was to notice what a returning plane is. It is a plane that took hits and still flew home. The holes on it are therefore a map of where a plane can be hit and survive. Planes hit in the places that show no holes — engine, cockpit, fuel lines — are not underrepresented in the data. They are the data that never arrived.

Damage on returning planes Naive reading Wald's reading
Fuselage and wings, densely holed Most vulnerable; armour here Survivable — these planes came back
Engine, cockpit, controls, rarely holed Safe; ignore Fatal — hits here produce no returning planes

The general lesson is portable and worth stating as a rule: the pattern of survivors tells you where the filter is survivable, not where the danger is. The same logic applies to a market's winners (the losers are gone from the index), a disease's diagnosed cases (the undiagnosed never entered the cohort), and a genre's classic albums (the bad ones stopped being played).

One honest footnote, in the spirit of the rest of this book: the popular telling compresses Wald's actual 1943 memorandum, which developed a formal method for estimating vulnerability from damage observed on survivors rather than delivering a single clever quip. The logic is exactly as described; the anecdote is a tidy version of a technical paper. Use it as an illustration, and cite it carefully.

The shapes it takes

Survivorship bias is a family, not a single error, and the variants require different corrections.

  • Silent failure. The failed cases existed and vanished from the record — closed funds, defunct firms, abandoned drugs, discontinued product lines. The fix is to find an archive; the difficulty is that no one is obliged to keep one.
  • Invisible success. Cases that passed the filter but never entered your sample because they were never visible in the first place — the successful small firms with no press coverage, the patients who recovered without ever seeing a doctor. This is non-participation rather than death, and it biases in the opposite direction from what you expect.
  • Selection on the outcome. This is the most analytically damaging variant: choosing your sample by the thing you want to explain, then hunting for its causes. Study "great companies" and identify what they had in common, and you will find things they shared with companies that failed. The shared traits were not causes; they were the entry ticket.
  • Selection on the observer. The advice you are given is, by construction, from someone for whom the advice worked — or someone lucky enough to think it did. The unsuccessful practitioner is not on the conference circuit.

Worked example: the fund that survived

A database of 1,000 mutual funds reports the returns of the funds still reporting after twenty years. The surviving funds averaged, say, 8% a year — a figure that quietly becomes "the stock market returns 8%."

But suppose roughly half of the original thousand closed or merged along the way, and those funds averaged closer to 4% in the years before they folded. An investor choosing at random in the first year did not get the database's 8%. They got a blend of the survivors' 8% and the dead funds' losses — plausibly closer to 5–6%, with a meaningful chance of having picked one of the funds that disappeared entirely.

Two details make this worse than it looks. First, the missing funds are missing non-randomly: funds close when they perform badly, so the absence correlates precisely with the variable of interest. Second, the loss is not just in the average but in the tail. The distribution of outcomes you can observe is truncated exactly where the bad outcomes were, which is why survivorship bias is a close cousin of the fat-tail problems discussed later. This is why regulators in several jurisdictions require fund companies to disclose survivorship-adjusted figures: the correction has to be imposed institutionally, because no individual has an incentive to volunteer it.

Why nobody keeps the graveyard

This is where survivorship bias stops being a cognitive quirk and becomes a coordination problem, which is why it belongs in this act.

An archive of failures is a public good. It is non-rival (everyone can use it), largely non-excludable (you cannot stop competitors learning from it), and — critically — nobody is paid to build it. Meanwhile the incentives run the other way at every level:

  • Failures have reasons to stay quiet. Reputation, legal exposure, commercial confidentiality, embarrassment. A closed fund's manager does not publish a post-mortem.
  • Successes have every reason to publish. Case studies, memoirs, keynote speeches, and bestsellers are all survivorship-powered genres. They are also genuinely useful, which is what makes them so persuasive.
  • The centre cannot see what leaves no trace. Failures are often illegible to any institution that could have recorded them: no filing, no headline, no attendance at the conference. The parties who know why something failed are the ones closest to it, and they disperse.
  • The counterfactual never arrives. There is no committee whose job is to ask what would have happened to the other nine hundred.

The result is a self-reinforcing loop, and it is worth following round:

As the traits spread through the population, they stop discriminating between success and failure at all — but the record still shows only winners, so they look universal and causal forever. The practical consequence is the one worth carrying: the more confidently a piece of advice is asserted, the more likely it has been sampled from survivors. Confidence is not evidence of a graveyard having been consulted. Usually the opposite.

Telling survivorship from a real effect

Three tests, in increasing order of effort:

Find the denominator. For any claim of the form "X leads to success," ask how many entities did X and failed. If you cannot construct that number, you have a numerator and no rate. This is the outside view applied to advice, and it is the same move as sourcing a prior from a reference class rather than from the vivid case in front of you.

Look for matched pairs. The strongest version of the test is comparative: take survivors who did X and failures who also did X. If most failures did X too, X explains nothing. Clinical research calls this a control group; business writing rarely bothers.

Ask what the failure of this claim would look like. If no observation could count against the advice — if the survivors' traits are unfalsifiable in the sense covered earlier in this book — then the claim has left the empirical game regardless of its provenance.

Two related habits keep the distinction sharp. First, distinguish persistence from merit: a convention can survive because of lock-in rather than because it is good, and a practice can survive because it is adequate. The Lindy effect says survival is information about durability, not about quality; survivorship bias is what you get when you read durability as approval. Second, remember that under a Bayesian lens, absent evidence is evidence when that evidence would otherwise have been likely to appear. A graveyard you cannot see, in a domain where corpses are usually visible, is itself a signal.

Antidotes

  • Name the filter before reading the data. Who had to pass something, and what happened to those who didn't?
  • Go and count the dead deliberately. Bankruptcy filings, retraction notices, clinical trial registries, discontinued product lines, obituaries of firms. Someone has to do this, and it is usually a research project rather than a dashboard.
  • Prefer registries to narratives. A registry — trial registration, pre-registration, mandatory fund disclosure — records entities at entry rather than at success. Pre-commitment is the institutional form of "I will count the failures too."
  • Read survivor accounts as testimony, not as data. They are one trajectory, selected on being interesting.
  • Invert the Wald question. Ask where the damage isn't, where the successful sample never ventures, and what that silence rules out.

Where it bites

  • Business and investing. Fund databases, "great company" studies, hedge fund indices, startup advice, "what I learned from failing" content that only reaches you when the failure was followed by a success.
  • Careers and education. The dropout-founder story, the graduate of an elite school, the person who moved cities and thrived. The base rate for each strategy is computed over a population whose failures are not giving talks.
  • Health and safety. Cats brought to veterinary clinics after falls, where cats that died on impact were never brought in; treatments evaluated only on patients who survived long enough to be evaluated; screening programmes that count detected cases rather than all cases.
  • Knowledge itself. Publication bias and the file-drawer problem are survivorship bias applied to findings: the literature contains the studies that reached significance, and the null results are the planes that did not come back. This is what pre-registration and registered reports are designed to fix.
  • Culture and taste. "The music of the 1970s was better," "they built things to last" — the past you can still see has been filtered by decades of selection, and the dross is not on the radio.

Terminology

  • Survivorship bias — Drawing conclusions from a sample that has been selected on the very outcome being explained, because cases that failed to pass a filter left no record.
  • Sampling frame — The rule determining which cases could appear in the data at all; the location of the bias.
  • The filter (gate) — The selection process — survival, publication, profitability, admission — that admits some cases and discards others.
  • Silent evidence — The absent cases whose absence is itself informative; a graveyard invisible from where you stand.
  • Selection on the outcome — Building a sample from entities that share a result, then searching for the causes of that result; the analytically worst variant of the bias.
  • Invisible survivor — A case that passed the filter but never entered the sample, because it was never visible or never reported.
  • Matched-pair (survivor–failure) comparison — Testing a claimed cause by checking whether failures shared it too; a control group by another name.
  • Denominator problem — Possessing a numerator (how many successes did X) without the base rate (how many did X and failed).
  • Survivorship-adjusted return — A performance figure corrected to include funds or firms that disappeared; mandated in some jurisdictions precisely because no one volunteers it.
  • File-drawer problem / publication bias — The same bias applied to research findings, where unpublished null results are the missing graveyard.
  • Persistence versus merit — The distinction between surviving because something resists elimination and surviving because it is good; conflating them is the error Lindy is often misused to commit.
