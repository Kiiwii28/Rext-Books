5-5 Norms vs. laws vs. markets

Part of 5 Act IV — The Coordination Problem (us)

Most coordination problems arrive with three candidate answers, and they are not three flavours of the same thing. Who gets the scarce lane at 8 a.m. can be settled by a queueing convention, by a traffic statute, or by a toll. Whether a river stays fishable can be settled by shared practice, by a regulator, or by a market in catch quotas. A norm, a law, and a market each make strangers behave predictably — but they use different detectors, different sanctions, different information, and they fail in different ways. Choosing among them is one of the few genuine design decisions available inside a complex system, and almost every real arrangement turns out to be all three stacked on top of one another.

One job, three mechanisms

All three answer the same four questions: what is the expected behaviour, who notices deviation, what happens on detection, and what does the apparatus cost to run. The differences show up in the third and fourth columns, and they are large.

Detector Sanction Information required Characteristic failure
Norm Whoever is watching — the affected party or bystanders Disapproval, gossip, exclusion, lost trust Local observability; repeated interaction; broadly shared expectations Doesn't scale past anonymity; can be oppressive; slow to change
Law A paid specialist — inspector, police, court, regulator Fine, liability, licence withdrawal, imprisonment Legibility: behaviour that can be stated in advance, proven, and attributed Costly per case; gameable once measured; misses what can't be written down
Market Nobody in particular — the loss arrives on its own Loss, bankruptcy, exclusion from purchase Excludability and enforceable ownership Misprices whatever can't be owned; distribution follows ability to pay

The deepest asymmetry is in the detector. A norm needs observability: if nobody sees the behaviour, there is no one to enforce. A law needs legibility: a rule that cannot be stated generally and proved afterwards cannot be enforced, however desirable it is — which is why the tacit knowledge that makes an organisation actually work tends to fall outside both the statute and the contract. A market needs neither, because no one has to notice anything: the cost lands directly on the person who incurred it, through the price.

Norms: everyone enforces, so nobody has to be paid

Norms are emergent order in its purest form. Nobody drafts them; they accumulate from repeated interaction and are enforced by whoever happens to be present. Enforcement is usually second-party (the person you inconvenienced) or third-party (a bystander who has no stake in this particular case), and its currency is reputational rather than material: disapproval, gossip, the withdrawal of cooperation, exclusion from the group.

That design has two enormous advantages. It is nearly free in the common case — the sanction is a look, and the deterrent is the anticipation of one — and it is extraordinarily fine-grained. A norm can encode local conditions that no rule-writer could enumerate: when queueing is expected, how much noise is acceptable at what hour, which seats you offer to whom. This is the tacit knowledge that formal systems keep failing to capture.

The conditions are as important as the benefits. Norms hold where behaviour is observable, where the parties expect to deal with each other again (the shadow of the future), and where the group broadly shares expectations about what is normal. Strip any of those out and enforcement evaporates. This is why norms govern village water use and not anonymous littering on a motorway: in the one-shot interaction with a stranger, there is no reputation to lose and nobody to lose it to.

Norms also fail in ways that are less discussed. They can be genuinely oppressive — many of the most costly norms in history were the ones allocating status, who belongs, and who may speak. They are path-dependent, so they can persist long after their reason has gone, and because they are inherited rather than written they are easy to mistake for human nature. And they resist deliberate change: you cannot legislate a norm, though you can sometimes legislate conditions in which one grows. What you can also do is destroy one, usually by accident.

Laws: enforcement by a specialist, paid for by everyone

Law is a made order — deliberately constructed, oriented to a stated purpose, held together by commands and the credible threat behind them. Its great advantage over the norm is that it works where norms cannot: between strangers, across one-shot interactions, at scale, and against harms that fall on third parties who were never part of the transaction. The owner of the paper mill does not need to be shamed by his neighbours, and his downstream neighbours do not need to negotiate with him repeatedly. They need an authority that will make him stop.

Three further advantages are worth naming. Law can be prospective, letting people plan against rules that exist before they act. It can function as a commitment device, binding the state or the public to a course that would otherwise be abandoned the moment it became inconvenient. And it produces dispute resolution that does not depend on the parties continuing to like each other.

The costs are equally structural. Enforcement is expensive and capacity is finite, which means every statute is in competition with every other for inspectors and court time. Law is slow to update relative to the conditions it governs. It is vulnerable to capture — the regulated usually have the strongest incentive to shape the rules, and the most resources with which to do it. And it decays under measurement in exactly the way the Goodhart discussion describes: police judged on arrest counts, regulators judged on fines levied, agencies judged on cases closed, all gradually optimising the number they can see instead of the outcome they were built to produce.

Above all, law depends on legibility. Writing a rule requires reducing a situation to terms that a general reader can apply and a court can verify. Whatever cannot survive that reduction is invisible to the statute — and so, very often, is the very thing that made the practice work.

Markets: enforcement by consequence, with no agreement on ends

Markets coordinate without anyone agreeing about what is worth wanting. That is their distinctive trick. Buyers and sellers who share no values, no language, and no intentions can still transact, because the only thing that has to be mutually intelligible is the price. And the price is a compression: a single number summarising scarcity, demand, and the local knowledge of thousands of people who never report to anyone — the mechanism explored under emergent versus designed order.

The information advantage is the one to hold onto. A market does not need to collect dispersed knowledge, only to transmit a signal about it, and each participant acts on their own private circumstances without explaining themselves to a centre. Its detector is automatic: nobody has to notice your error, because the loss arrives regardless.

Its failures are equally precise, and they all have the same shape — the signal is missing where nothing can be owned:

  • Externalities. Costs that land on people outside the transaction, who cannot charge for them: pollution, noise, congestion, a depleted aquifer.
  • Public goods. Things that are costly to provide and impossible to exclude from, so nobody's private incentive covers the cost.
  • Common-pool resources. Rivalrous but hard to fence — the subject of the tragedy of the commons and of Ostrom's escape from it.
  • Asymmetric information. When one side knows more about the thing being sold, the price stops clearing the market honestly.

Two further limits matter as much. First, markets need law and norms to function at all: property, contract, courts, credible money, and a background expectation that most people will not simply defraud you. Second, the allocation a market produces follows willingness to pay, and willingness to pay tracks wealth. That is a value choice presented as an emergent outcome — the is/ought gap with a price tag attached.

They are layered, not alternatives

Read the stack upwards and it explains why the three are not substitutes in any simple sense. A market cannot exist without enforceable property and contract. A legal system cannot function on coercion alone, because enforcement capacity is finite and most compliance in any functioning society is voluntary — a law nobody believes in is a surcharge on the honest. And the norms at the bottom are the ones markets and statutes quietly consume.

They also eat into each other. The best-known demonstration is the daycare study: a group of Haifa childcare centres introduced a small fine for parents who collected their children late. Lateness rose rather than fell, and — the part that matters — it stayed high after the fine was withdrawn. The explanation is a change of frame: a lateness that was a breach of consideration toward a person became a service purchased at a listed price, and parents optimised against the price. Withdrawing the price did not restore the norm, because the reframing did not reverse. The same argument runs through Titmuss's case against paying blood donors. One caveat, in keeping with everything this book has said about single studies: the effect is widely cited, the replications are mixed, and the honest reading is that the mechanism is credible and its magnitude unsettled. Treat it as a live hypothesis to test in your own context, not a law of nature.

Worked example: the eight a.m. lane

One scarce resource, three possible regimes.

  • Norm. First-come-first-served, with a body of tacit merging etiquette — let one car in, don't block the junction, don't use the shoulder. Nearly free to run, and it degrades gracefully under light load. It collapses at saturation, when nobody can see who arrived when and every driver can plausibly claim priority.
  • Law. Right-of-way rules, signals, a bus lane with a penalty, a box junction you may not enter. Costs inspectors and cameras, works among strangers, and can reserve capacity for people who were never going to win a queue — which is itself a value judgement written into the rule.
  • Market. Congestion charging, tolled express lanes, priced parking. Clears the lane and raises revenue, allocates strictly by ability to pay, and generates a political problem with a familiar shape: the losers are concentrated and identifiable, the winners are diffuse.

Notice that all three settle the same question — who gets the space — and settle it differently. The efficiency question, which clears traffic fastest and at what total cost, is empirical and answerable. The distribution question, who should bear the cost and who should get through, is not settled by any amount of evidence; it needs a bridge premise about what is worth wanting, supplied from somewhere other than the data.

There is a second lesson in that example. London's congestion charge works mainly because most drivers pay it voluntarily. The cameras are a backstop, not the mechanism; enforcement of a charge against a population that has decided to ignore it is not affordable at any realistic budget. Every market and every statute ultimately stands on a floor of voluntary compliance, which is the bottom layer of the diagram above.

Choosing, and mixing

The questions that actually decide it:

  • What is the information structure? Dispersed, local, and tacit favours norms and markets. Central, provable, and general favours law.
  • Who can see the behaviour? No observability, no norm. No legibility, no enforceable rule.
  • Who bears the enforcement cost? Norms look free until the group gets large; law is expensive per case and needs a budget.
  • Do the parties disagree about ends or only means? Markets coordinate people who agree on nothing; norms require enough shared expectation to define a violation.
  • Will the mechanism destroy the thing it regulates? Pricing and fining can reframe moral obligations as commercial ones, and the reframing is not cheap to undo.

Three practical rules follow. Prefer the mechanism that works at the lowest total cost, including the cost of monitoring it. Expect hybrids, and design them deliberately — a price with a norm underneath and a statute behind it is usually stronger than any one alone. And schedule a review: every mechanism decays, markets attract rent-seekers, laws drift under their own metrics, and norms change in ways nobody announced.

When none of the three can be reached — when the only stable arrangement requires everyone to restrain themselves simultaneously and no one can enforce the restraint — the result is a race to the bottom that no participant wants. Law is the usual escape from that trap, which is why the politics of enforcement matters more than the elegance of the design.

Terminology

  • Coordination mechanism — any arrangement that aligns the behaviour of independent actors; here, norms, laws, and markets are treated as three distinct technologies for doing so.
  • Norm — an unwritten expectation enforced by those present, through disapproval, gossip, exclusion, or lost trust.
  • Law — a formal, prospective, generally stated rule enforced by a specialised authority with coercive power.
  • Market — a coordination mechanism in which behaviour is disciplined by prices, profit, loss, and the ability to exit.
  • Observability — the property of behaviour being visible to those who might sanction it; the precondition for norm enforcement.
  • Legibility — the property of behaviour being expressible as a general, verifiable rule; the precondition for legal enforcement.
  • First-, second-, and third-party enforcement — enforcement by the actor themselves, by the affected party, or by an uninvolved bystander; norms rely mainly on the second and third.
  • Shadow of the future — the expectation of dealing with the same parties again, which makes present defection costly and gives norms their grip.
  • Externality — a cost or benefit falling on someone outside the transaction, who cannot charge for it; the characteristic market failure.
  • Public good — a benefit that is costly to provide and impossible to exclude people from consuming, so private incentives under-supply it.
  • Willingness to pay — the criterion markets allocate by; tracks wealth, and so makes distribution a value question rather than a technical one.
  • Motivation crowding out — the erosion of a norm when a price or fine reframes a moral obligation as a paid transaction.
  • Regulatory capture — the tendency of the regulated to shape the rules that govern them, since they have the strongest incentive and the most resources.
  • Mixed regime — a real arrangement combining all three mechanisms, typically with the market layer resting on law and both resting on voluntary compliance.
