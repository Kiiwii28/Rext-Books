6-2 Discounting & the far future

Part of 6 Act V — Living With It (synthesis)

Open almost any spreadsheet used to justify a long-lived project and you will find one number doing more work than all the others combined. Change the discount rate from 3% to 7% and a benefit due in a hundred years loses a factor of fifty in value — not because anything about the future changed, but because of a line in a cell. That single parameter decides whether a dam, a forest, a pension promise, a climate policy, or a species is worth protecting, and it is almost never argued for out loud. This subsection is about where that number comes from, why it is a value choice wearing the costume of an interest rate, and what to do when you are the one who has to pick it.

The same curve, run backwards

Act III introduced exponential growth as the thing human intuition refuses to feel. Discounting is that same exponential with a minus sign on the rate.

Compounding says: £1 now becomes about £1,000 in a century at 7%. Discounting says exactly the reverse: £1,000 in a century is worth about 98 pence today. The rule of 72 from the deep-time material applies unchanged — 7% halves the value every ten years, so a hundred years is ten halvings, and 2⁻¹⁰ ≈ 0.001.

The present value of a future benefit is what you would have to invest today, at the assumed rate, to end up with that benefit. Formally, PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ. This is not a trick or a fudge; it is the correct answer to a well-posed question: if the alternative is putting the money in something that earns r, what is this promise worth?

Everything interesting lies in which question you are actually asking.

Two questions that look identical

Question one: the firm's question. I can spend £1M on a machine that returns £1.6M in ten years, or put the £1M in an index fund earning 7%. The machine is worth £813,000 in present-value terms, so I should buy the fund. Here, r is an opportunity cost, and using the market rate is not a preference — it is arithmetic. Nothing ethical is happening.

Question two: the generation's question. I can spend £1M on coastal defences that will spare people in 2126 a cost of £1.6M, or spend it on hospital beds now. Here, r is doing something entirely different: it is a statement about how much less a person's welfare counts for the sole reason that they will be born later. That is not arithmetic. It is a bridge premise in the sense Act II used the term — a normative assumption that has to be stated and defended, and which no amount of data can supply.

The catastrophic habit is to let the answer to question one be smuggled into question two — to say "the discount rate is 7% because that's the market rate" when the actual claim being made is "the suffering of people a century from now counts for a thousandth of yours." That is the is/ought gap wearing a suit, and it is the single most consequential instance of it in public policy.

The Ramsey decomposition: two dials, not one

Frank Ramsey's 1928 formulation separates the rate into the two things actually going on:

  • δ (delta) — the pure rate of time preference. This is discounting for the sole reason that a benefit arrives later. Ramsey's own verdict on it has never really been improved: discounting later enjoyments merely because they are later is "a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination."
  • η (eta) — the elasticity of marginal utility, or how fast an extra pound loses usefulness as you get richer. A pound to a rich person buys less welfare than a pound to a poor one.
  • g — the growth rate of consumption per person.

The decomposition does real work, because only the first dial is ethically loaded. The second is not discounting at all in the moral sense — it says that a marginal pound buys less welfare in a richer world, which is true for the same reason that pounds buy less welfare across income levels today. Even with δ = 0 — a flat refusal to privilege the present — the rate stays positive whenever growth is positive. Growth is what makes the future cheap in money, not a preference for the present.

The practical payoff: when someone quotes "the" discount rate, you can ask which dial it came from. If the entire number is δ, you are being asked to accept that later people matter less. That may be defensible — most people do discount their own futures slightly, and institutions that don't discount at all behave strangely — but it needs saying.

Worked example: Stern against Nordhaus

The most instructive dispute in the modern literature is the one between Nicholas Stern's 2006 Review on the Economics of Climate Change and William Nordhaus's response to it. They used the same framework and reached opposite policy conclusions.

Stern (2006) Nordhaus
Pure time preference (δ) 0.1% — essentially zero, justified only by a small probability of human extinction Around 3% — read off observed behaviour and market returns
Elasticity (η) 1 1–2
Growth (g) 1.3% 1.3%
Implied rate (r) ≈ 1.4% ≈ 4–6%
Policy conclusion Immediate, aggressive abatement Gradual, ramping abatement

Watch how little separates them and how much follows. On the empirical side — how fast the economy grows, what damages look like — they largely agree. The entire disagreement is about δ: whether near-term consumption is worth pursuing for its own sake because it is near.

At 1.4%, a 2% loss of global output in 2100 has a substantial present value. At 5%, the same loss shrinks toward irrelevance, and the model dutifully recommends that the cheapest course is to grow richer now and pay to adapt later. The model is not lying. It is faithfully reporting the ethical premise it was fed.

That is the point to carry: a disagreement that presents itself as technical is often a value dispute in which the value is a parameter. Locating it — in one cell of a spreadsheet — is the premise audit from Act II applied to the most consequential number in economics.

Uncertainty bends the curve

There is a genuinely surprising result that rescues you from having to settle the Stern–Nordhaus argument before you can plan. Martin Weitzman's gamma discounting (2001): if you are uncertain about the correct discount rate — it might be 1%, it might be 5% — then the certainty-equivalent rate you should use is not the average. It declines as the horizon extends, converging toward the lowest plausible rate.

The mechanism is the compounding arithmetic from Act III, applied to uncertainty. Weight the two scenarios by their probabilities, and at long horizons the 1% branch contributes vastly more present value than the 5% branch, because it compounds the discount slower. So the effective rate you should apply is a declining sequence: perhaps 4% for a decade, 3% for fifty years, approaching 1% for a millennium.

This is why several governments — including the United Kingdom, France, and Norway — now use declining discount rates in official project appraisal rather than one flat rate. It is one of the few places where a philosophical dispute has been partially defused by a probabilistic argument, and it fits the pattern this book keeps returning to: uncertainty does not require you to resolve the question, only to be honest about the distribution of answers.

Two cautions. First, Weitzman's own later dismal theorem (2009) argued that with sufficiently heavy-tailed uncertainty about catastrophic outcomes, expected-value reasoning may fail altogether — the expectation can be dominated by events so extreme that no finite estimate is meaningful. Do not take a declining rate as a licence to relax; take it as evidence that this is fat-tailed territory. Second, a declining rate schedule is still a choice about the shape, and can be gamed by choosing a schedule that produces the answer you wanted.

The personal version: hyperbolic discounting

The same structure appears inside one skull, and it is worth naming because it is the most immediately actionable form.

Human beings do not discount exponentially. Experiments consistently show hyperbolic discounting — very steep for the near term, much shallower for the far term — which produces preference reversal. Offered £100 today or £120 next month, most people take the £100. Offered £100 in twelve months or £120 in thirteen, most people wait. The same twelve-month delay, valued differently depending on where it sits. That is time inconsistency: your preferences today and your preferences-in-twelve-months are not the same preferences.

The response is the commitment device — the Ulysses contract. Automatic pension enrolment, standing orders, a savings rule deducted before the money is visible, a deadline made public. These are institutions at the scale of one person, and they work for exactly the reasons institutional commitment devices work at scale: they change the payoffs facing a future self who would otherwise prefer to defect.

This connects directly to the dual-process and institutions material. Present bias is not a character flaw; it is the default setting of hardware that evolved for immediate, personal risk. The fix is structural — the same conclusion the rest of the book keeps reaching.

Where it bites

  • Climate policy — the discount rate has done more to shape policy conclusions than any physical estimate, which is why the Stern–Nordhaus dispute matters beyond its own field.
  • Infrastructure — a bridge, rail line, or dam is justified or rejected by the terminal value assigned to benefits beyond the appraisal window, which is a number chosen by convention more often than by argument.
  • Nuclear waste and long-lived hazards — the deep-time material is the mirror image: institutions planning markers for 10,000 years are operating outside any horizon an ordinary discount rate can represent, which is precisely why they require a mandated rather than a computed answer.
  • Pensions and sovereign debt — promises across decades, discounted at rates that flatter whoever is currently in office.
  • Healthcare prioritisation — where a QALY in fifty years is implicitly worth a fraction of one today, usually without anyone deciding that.
  • Biodiversity and extinction — irreversible losses are structurally invisible to net-present-value analysis, because the loss is not a flow that can be discounted but a stock that has been eliminated.
  • Your own career and health — the multi-decade compounding in both directions: the body you will have at seventy, and the skills that compound or decay.

Running the drill

When you face a decision with a long tail, six moves do most of the work.

  1. Separate the dials. Write the rate as δ + ηg. Only the first needs ethical defence; the second is a factual claim about future wealth that you can test.
  2. Say your δ out loud. "I am using 3% pure time preference, which means I am asserting that a person born in 2100 counts for about a twentieth of a person alive today over that horizon." If the sentence is unbearable when spoken, the number is wrong.
  3. Run the sensitivity before the analysis. Compute the answer at 0%, 1.5%, 3%, and 7%. If the conclusion flips between two adjacent rates, the discount rate is the argument, and you should be arguing about it rather than presenting a result.
  4. Show the undiscounted quantity alongside. "£1.6M of damage in 2126, present value £2,000" keeps both the map and the territory visible in the same sentence — the cheapest possible inoculation against reification.
  5. Prefer a declining schedule and say why. It is the honest representation of your uncertainty and it is now standard practice in several governments. If you use a flat rate, note that you are asserting something a declining rate denies.
  6. Choose behind a veil. You do not know which generation you will be born into. Ask what rate you would accept if you did not know whether you were the beneficiary in 2026 or the payer in 2226. This is the veil of ignorance from the previous subsection, and it is the only test that reliably catches a rate chosen to flatter its author.

Two further habits prevent the drill from becoming a way of doing nothing. Act on the robust moves regardless of the rate — anything that is worth doing at 1%, 3%, and 7% is worth doing without settling the argument, and reversibility is the property that makes a decision safe to take under deep uncertainty. And name the settlement date even when it is far off: a claim about the year 2126 cannot be checked, but a claim that "this policy will be extended after its first five-year review" can.

The uncomfortable implication of this whole subsection is that the far future is not neglected because people are callous. It is neglected because our tools for thinking about it have a built-in parameter that erases it, and the parameter usually goes unexamined. Compounding and discounting are one curve; the direction you run it in is a choice, and it is the clearest case in this book of a technical-looking number that is really a statement about what matters.

Terminology

Discount rate — The rate r at which a future benefit is reduced to its present value; conventionally the consumption discount rate in social appraisals.

Present value — The amount that would have to be invested today, at the assumed rate, to yield a given future amount; FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ.

Pure time preference (δ) — The component of the discount rate that values a benefit less purely because it arrives later; the ethically contested dial.

Elasticity of marginal utility (η) — The rate at which an additional unit of consumption loses welfare value as consumption rises; governs how much the growing wealth of future people reduces the weight on their welfare.

Ramsey equation — r = δ + ηg; the decomposition of the discount rate into a pure time preference and a diminishing-returns term.

Consumption discount rate — The rate applied to welfare-relevant consumption across time, as distinct from the market interest rate facing a particular investor.

Opportunity cost rate — The return available on the best alternative use of funds; the appropriate rate for commercial appraisal, and not automatically appropriate for intergenerational welfare questions.

Hyperbolic discounting — Discounting that is steep in the near term and shallow in the far term, unlike the constant proportional reduction of exponential discounting.

Time inconsistency / preference reversal — The property that today's preference over two future options differs from the preference that will be held when the earlier option arrives.

Commitment device (Ulysses contract) — An arrangement that removes a future option in order to make a present plan credible, whether at personal scale (automatic saving) or institutional scale (a mandate, an entrenched rule).

Gamma discounting / declining discount rate (Weitzman) — The result that uncertainty about the true discount rate implies a certainty-equivalent rate that falls as the horizon extends, approaching the lowest plausible rate.

Dismal theorem (Weitzman) — The argument that with sufficiently heavy-tailed uncertainty about catastrophic outcomes, expected-value analysis may be dominated by unmodellable extremes and cease to be informative.

Certainty-equivalent rate — The single rate whose application yields the same value as the probability-weighted average of several possible rates over a given horizon.

Terminal value — The value assigned to all benefits beyond the explicit appraisal period; frequently the largest and least examined number in a long-horizon cost-benefit analysis.

Irreversibility — The property of a loss (extinction, aquifer depletion) that cannot be represented as a flow and therefore cannot be meaningfully discounted.

Discounted at the market rate — The practice of applying an observed financial return to an intergenerational welfare question, converting an opportunity cost into an implicit claim that future people matter less.

Sensitivity analysis — Recomputing a result across a range of discount rates and other contested parameters to identify which one the conclusion actually hangs on.

Veil-of-ignorance test for the rate — Choosing a discount rate without knowing which generation one will inhabit; a consistency check on rates that advantage their author.
