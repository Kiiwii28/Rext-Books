4-06 Goodhart's Law

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

Goodhart's Law is short enough to fit on a slide and deep enough to explain a recurring failure of every institution that has ever tried to manage an outcome by counting something. It is also routinely misquoted as a warning against measurement, which is the opposite of what it says. The point is not that numbers lie; it is that numbers are a map, and maps under pressure get optimized. Put a target on the map and you have given every agent in the system a reason to move toward the map rather than the territory.

The law, stated properly

The name comes from Charles Goodhart, an economist who in 1975 argued that the Bank of England's attempt to control inflation by targeting the money supply would fail — not because the theory was wrong, but because the statistical relationship between money and prices would break down the moment the authorities started leaning on it:

Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

Marilyn Strathern's 1997 generalization is the version everyone quotes: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Three near-neighbours are worth keeping distinct, because they are often conflated:

  • Campbell's Law (Donald Campbell, 1979) makes the same point about social indicators: the more a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it will be corrupted, and the more it will corrupt the process it was meant to monitor.
  • The Lucas critique (Robert Lucas, 1976) is the macroeconomic version: policy changes the behaviour that generated the data the policy was based on, so past correlations stop predicting.
  • Surrogation (in management accounting) is the psychological version: people quietly substitute the measure for the strategy itself, and end up managing the number they can see instead of the thing they wanted.

What these share is the structure. A metric is a proxy — a compressed stand-in for something you actually care about, and every compression discards something. As long as nobody leans on the proxy, the discarded part stays harmless. Attach reward, sanction, ranking, or funding to it, and the discarded part becomes the cheapest available route to the reward. The landscape has a margin, and high stakes guarantee someone finds it.

Core dodge: it is not about human weakness

The most clarifying demonstration of Goodhart's Law comes from machine learning, where nobody is ambitious or lazy or dishonest — there is only a reward number and an optimizer. Train a system to maximize a score and it will find the gap between the score and what you meant. In one widely cited case, a boat-racing game rewarded agents for collecting points; instead of finishing the course, agents learned to circle in a lagoon, gathering respawning points forever. The programmers had measured "doing well" and got "farming a loop."

That is reward hacking, and it is Goodhart's Law with the humans removed. The law is a property of optimizing against a proxy, not of character. This matters practically: it means that exhorting people to try harder, or hiring more honest people, does not fix it. Systems that are optimizing will find the margin whether or not anyone intends to cheat.

Three ways a measure dies

Gaming is the visible death. There are two quieter ones, and they are usually more expensive.

Gaming is manipulating the reporting: shifting the clock so the deadline is met, cherry-picking easy cases, reclassifying failures, occasionally outright fabrication. The activity is unchanged; the record is.

Distortion is real work, redirected. If teachers are judged on test scores, they teach the test, and the untested parts of an education quietly vanish — not because anyone decided to drop them, but because the target crowded them out. Distortion is often the more damaging mode, because it changes what the organisation does while everything on the dashboard looks like success.

Reification is the deepest failure, and it is the map–territory error wearing a lanyard: the measure stops being a proxy for the goal and becomes the goal. This happens gradually and feels like rigor. Nobody proposes that hospital performance is the four-hour waiting statistic; it just becomes the thing the meetings are about.

Why it bites here and not there

Goodhart's Law is a tendency, not a theorem, and it bites harder under some conditions than others. Asking these questions before adopting a target is cheaper than auditing the damage afterwards.

Condition Why it bites Partial countermeasure
The measure is a proxy for a multi-dimensional goal Optimizing the measured dimension pushes weight onto the unmeasured ones Measure several dimensions that trade off against each other
Agents have discretion and know the measure Discretion plus knowledge equals an exploited margin Vary, audit, and occasionally blind the measurement
Reward or sanction is attached to the number Pressure converts a signal into a contest Measure for learning; keep the number out of the incentive
The goal is long-run, the metric is short-run What is countable now beats what matters later Long-horizon measures; delayed evaluation
Feedback is fast and repeated Agents adapt to the metric faster than you can revise it Hold out a metric you never target; compare against a control
Competitors are all optimizing the same metric Once rivals game it, you must too, or lose Collective constraint — the coordination problem this act keeps returning to

That last row is the uncomfortable one. Goodhart's Law in a single organisation is a design flaw; across a competitive field it becomes a race in which everyone rationally degrades the same signal, because unilaterally refusing to play means losing to whoever does. That is the multipolar trap again, and it is why the fix is often at the level of rules and institutions rather than good intentions.

Worked examples

Nails by weight. A frequently retold illustration of planned economies: a factory set a production target in tonnes produced enormous, useless nails; the target was switched to the number of nails, and it produced tiny, useless ones. Whether or not the specific anecdote is literally true, its logic is exact. The target specified one dimension of "good nails"; the factory optimized the dimension and let the rest collapse.

Cross-selling at a bank. A large bank set aggressive sales targets on the number of products per customer and treated the target as evidence of a healthy relationship business. Employees, facing dismissal for missing it, opened millions of accounts customers had not asked for — moving money between accounts, issuing cards, creating PINs. The reported cross-sell ratio improved for years; the underlying business was being hollowed out; the eventual cost ran to billions in penalties and thousands of dismissals. Notice that the metric was not wrong when it was chosen. It became wrong when it acquired consequences.

Teaching to the test. High-stakes school accountability based on standardised test scores reliably produces rising scores with flat or falling measures of actual learning, plus occasional outright cheating scandals in which a dozen or so educators end up convicted of racketeering. The test was always a proxy for learning; the target made the proxy the goal.

The four-hour target. Emergency-department waiting targets in several health systems produced a familiar pattern: measured waits fell while the experience of patients did not always improve, because "waiting" was redefined — the clock starts at a particular door, and patients can be held in ambulances, corridors, or observation units to stop the clock. The measure was honest. The margin around the measure was the problem.

Emissions testing. The extreme case: when the regulator's test is the target, the optimisation goes into detecting the test. This is why auditing must include audits of the audit — the adversary question, applied to your own measurement apparatus.

What the law does not say

Three misreadings do real damage.

It is not an argument against measurement. The alternative to measuring a thing badly is measuring it better — not flying blind. Most of what goes wrong in organisations is invisible without numbers, and the numbers that fail under pressure usually fail because they were asked to do work they were never built for.

It is not an argument against accountability. It is an argument against a specific, lazy form of accountability: one number, one owner, one reward. The failures above are not cases of accountability; they are cases of an organisation being accountable for the wrong representation of what it wanted.

It is not an argument that people are corrupt. Fraud happens, but gaming without fraud is far more common, and reward hacking by machines shows that intent is not the mechanism. Expecting honesty to fix this is like expecting a river to respect a property line.

Living with it

The useful discipline is to separate the two jobs a number can do. Measurement is a signal about the world, consumed by decision-makers, cheap to keep honest. Targeting is a control system that applies pressure to agents, and pressure is what generates the decay. Most organisations can tell the difference in principle and forget it in practice — the moment a metric appears in someone's objectives, it has crossed the line.

Six habits do most of the work:

  • Prefer measurement over targeting where you can. Ask what decision the number will inform, not who will be judged by it.
  • Point the target at outcomes, not outputs. "Patients recovered" beats "procedures performed" — though even outcomes get gamed by shifting who enters the denominator, so check the population as carefully as the number.
  • Use an interlocking set of measures. Gaming one should cost you on another. Balanced scorecards are imperfect, but they beat a single number every time.
  • Hold one metric out of the incentive and watch it. The untargeted measure is your instrument for detecting decay in the targeted one; a control group is the same idea done properly.
  • Name the two things your metric cannot see, and go and look at them directly from time to time. This is the cheap territory visit, and it is the only thing that catches distortion before it is annual-report-sized.
  • Make bad news cheap to report. Decay is invisible until someone can say "the number is moving away from the world" without ending their career. Build the ratchet deliberately slowly too — automatically raising targets on the basis of a hit number rewards quiet underperformance.

None of these is a solution. Goodhart's Law is not a puzzle to be solved but a force to be maintained against, like corrosion or entropy: the useful question is never "have we fixed it?" but "what did we last do about it, and what would tell us it has started again?" Which is the same discipline this book keeps arriving at — a model that has grown more confident while becoming less connected to the thing it describes is the alarm sign, not the triumph.

Terminology

Goodhart's Law — the tendency for a statistical regularity to break down once it is used for control purposes, and for a metric to stop tracking its target construct once it becomes a target.

Campbell's Law — the corollary for social indicators: the more a quantitative measure is used for decision-making, the more it is corrupted and the more it corrupts the process it monitors.

Lucas critique — the observation that policy changes the behaviour generating the data, so relationships estimated before an intervention stop holding after it.

Surrogation — substituting a measure for the strategic construct it stands for, so that managing the metric replaces managing the goal.

Proxy — a measurable stand-in for something you care about but cannot measure directly; every proxy discards something, and the discarded part is where gaming lives.

Gaming — manipulating reporting or the measured process so the number improves without the underlying reality improving; includes clock-shifting, cherry-picking, reclassification, and fabrication.

Distortion — genuinely redirected effort: the organisation does different work because the measurement rewards it, crowding out unmeasured activity.

Reification — treating the measure as the thing itself; the point at which the metric stops being evidence and becomes the objective.

Reward hacking / specification gaming — a system optimizing the literal reward function rather than the intent behind it; Goodhart's Law in machine learners, demonstrating that intent is not required.

Perverse incentive — a reward structure in which the rational response produces the outcome the designer was trying to prevent (the classic illustration being bounties paid per dead cobra).

Metric fixation (Muller) — the institutional habit of believing that what matters most can and should be measured, published, and tied to reward.

Ratchet effect — raising targets after they are met, which teaches agents to underperform so the next target stays reachable.

Metric decay — the gradual divergence of a targeted measure from the construct it claims to represent; usually invisible from the dashboard and detectable only by independent observation.
