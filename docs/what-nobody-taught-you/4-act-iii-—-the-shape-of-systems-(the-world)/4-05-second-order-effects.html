4-05 Second-order effects

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

Most of the effects we argue about are the ones we were aiming at. A new tax raises revenue; a dam generates power; a safety rule reduces injuries. The interesting effects are almost never these. They are the ones produced by the system's response to what we did — the consequences of the consequences. Second-order effects are where interventions go to die, and where the reputation of good intentions is quietly settled.

The orders, and why the label matters

The vocabulary is borrowed from mathematics, where an nth-order effect is the effect of the (n−1)th-order effect acting on something else. Applied to interventions:

  • First-order effect: the direct, intended change, in the thing you targeted, on the timescale you had in mind. A new antibiotic kills the bacteria infecting this patient. Widening a road shortens the trip for the drivers currently on it.
  • Second-order effect: what happens because that first-order change altered someone's behaviour, a price, or a stock. Resistance evolves. The faster road attracts new drivers. The effect is often not in the target system at all — it is in the things connected to it.
  • Third-order effect: what happens when the second-order effect changes the rules of the system: norms, expectations, institutions, laws. Resistance becomes a norm of hospital prescribing; induced traffic reshapes land use, which locks in car dependence for a generation.

This is distinct from the loose phrase "side effect." A side effect is usually a first-order consequence you didn't intend — the drug also makes you drowsy. A second-order effect is a consequence of the intended consequence, generated by the system adapting to your intervention. That distinction matters, because side effects are usually visible in the same study, and second-order effects almost never are.

The general phenomenon has a respectable ancestry: Robert Merton's 1936 essay on the unanticipated consequences of purposive social action identified ignorance, error, an "imperious immediacy of interest" (wanting the near result so badly that the long term is discounted away), and self-defeating prophecy as the recurring causes. He was writing about social policy; the mechanism is identical in medicine, ecology, and engineering.

Why the later orders go unseen

Four features conspire to hide them, and all four are properties of the system rather than of the observer's diligence:

Delay. The second-order effect arrives after the evaluation window closes. Antibiotic resistance takes years to become a crisis; induced traffic takes a decade to show up as a congested ring road. If your metric is measured at six months, the loop has not yet finished a lap.

Diffusion. The benefit is concentrated in the people you helped; the cost is spread thinly across everyone else. This is the shape of nearly every policy trap — the winners are identifiable and motivated, the losers are statistical and unaware. It is also why the political cost of a second-order effect is systematically underweighted.

Attribution. When the second-order effect arrives, it arrives as a new problem with its own proximate cause. Nobody in 1960 attributes the 1990 firestorm to the successful fire suppression of 1950. The causal chain is real but not perceptible from inside a single episode.

Positive feedback. Some second-order effects don't just add to the first-order effect — they amplify it, which is what happens when the response feeds back into the original cause. This is the bridge to the chapter on feedback loops: second-order effects are the mechanism by which a one-time intervention becomes a self-sustaining dynamic.

Four engines that generate them

Adaptation. People, firms, pests, and pathogens respond to changed incentives. This is the single most reliable source. If an intervention makes something cheaper, expect more of it; if it makes something punishable, expect substitution toward whatever is not yet punished.

Rebound and efficiency. Making a thing more efficient lowers its effective cost, which increases consumption of it. Jevons noticed in 1865 that more efficient coal engines increased total coal use rather than reducing it. The same pattern recurs in fuel economy, computing, and healthcare capacity — a hospital bed freed is a hospital bed filled.

Displacement. The problem moves rather than shrinks. Ban a drug in one port and it lands in another; block traffic on the arterial and it appears on the residential streets; suppress fires in the forest and the fuel load builds up elsewhere in the same system.

Risk compensation. Safety measures that change perceived risk can change behaviour to offset some of the gain — the contested "Peltzman effect" around seat belts and airbags. The honest treatment: the effect is real and measurable in some safety domains (mandatory helmets, ski bindings), smaller than critics claim in others, and hotly disputed in the case of seat belts specifically. Include it as a hypothesis to test, not a law to cite.

Worked example: the forest that burned because we put the fires out

Take the most instructive case, because the intervention was good, the first-order effect was real, and the second-order effect was catastrophic.

The intervention. From the early twentieth century, fire agencies in the United States adopted total suppression. Every fire, as fast as possible. The US Forest Service's "10 a.m. policy" — a fire should be controlled by 10 a.m. the following morning — was the operational expression of it, reinforced by the Smokey Bear campaign from 1944.

First-order effect. It worked. Wildfire acreage fell sharply across the western United States for decades. That is not a claim in dispute; suppression suppressed fire. Any evaluation ending here would rate the programme an unambiguous success.

Second-order effect. Fire is not only a destroyer in these ecosystems; it is a consumer of fuel. Suppress it, and dead wood, brush, and litter accumulate faster than they decompose. Forests that historically burned in frequent, low-intensity fires — which cleared undergrowth and left mature trees standing — became loaded with fuel. Insects and drought added dead trees. When fire finally arrived, it no longer crept; it crowned.

Third-order effect. The system reorganised around the fix. Communities were built in the newly fire-free wildland-urban interface, which raised the stakes of every future fire. Agencies staffed and budgeted for suppression rather than fuel management. A whole set of norms — "fire is the enemy," "all fires are preventable" — hardened into policy, and unwinding them means arguing against a public that remembers only the fires.

The loop above is reinforcing, not balancing: each round of suppression makes the next fire worse, which triggers more suppression. That is the signature of a second-order effect that has become self-reinforcing, and it is the reason prescribed burns and managed wildfire, now routine practice, are politically difficult even though the ecology is not really in dispute.

The shape of the pattern, across domains

Intervention First-order effect Second-order effect Engine
Antibiotics, widely used Infections cured Resistance evolves; resistant strains spread Adaptation
Road widening Congestion eases Induced demand refills the road; land use spreads out Rebound
Cobra bounty (the cautionary tale) Cobras killed People breed cobras for the bounty Perverse incentive
Fire suppression Fires stopped Fuel builds; fires become catastrophic Displacement + delay
"Arrest more of X" X arrests rise Police attention shifts from Y; Y rises Displacement
Standardised tests as a school metric Measurable outcomes improve Teaching narrows to the test Goodhart

Two cautions on the table. First, the cobra bounty story — colonial Hanoi paying per cobra head, residents farming cobras — is widely repeated and thinly sourced. It is a good mnemonic and a poor citation, and the honest move is to use it as an illustration while noting that the general pattern is documented many times over. Second, the size of the second-order effect is not fixed. Sometimes it is trivial; sometimes — fire suppression, antibiotic resistance — it swamps the first-order benefit and reverses the sign of the whole intervention. That variability is what makes this a systems concept rather than a rule of thumb: the outcome depends on the loop structure, not on the strength of the original intention.

Where second-order reasoning goes wrong

This is a discipline, not a mood, and it has two characteristic failure modes.

Infinite regress and paralysis. Every intervention has consequences, and those have consequences, so by induction nothing should ever be done. That argument is empty: the status quo is itself an intervention with its own second-order effects, and "do nothing" merely means someone else's earlier fix continues to run. The rule is proportionality — the depth of analysis should match the stakes and the reversibility — plus a steady recognition that inaction is a choice with a beneficiary.

Special pleading for the status quo. "Unintended consequences" is the most convenient argument in politics precisely because it is unfalsifiable in the moment. Where an advocate waves at hypothetical downstream harm without naming the mechanism, the timescale, or the observation that would settle it, treat it as rhetoric rather than analysis. The test is the same one used everywhere in this book: name in advance what you expect to happen, and when you would know.

A practical drill

  • Ask "and then what?" three times. Not indefinitely — three steps is enough to surface adaptation, rebound, and displacement in most cases.
  • Find the loop. Does the first-order effect change a price, a stock, or a rule that feeds back into the original cause? If yes, model the loop, not the line.
  • Ask who adapts, and how. Then ask whether their adaptation is inside your evaluation window. If it isn't, your evaluation is measuring the wrong thing.
  • Check displacement. Is the problem solved, or moved? Follow the people and the load, not the boundary of the jurisdiction.
  • Watch the metric. The moment a measure becomes a target, its second-order effect is already underway.
  • Set a settlement date and a sunset clause. Reversibility is the cheapest insurance against a second-order effect you didn't foresee. A policy that expires unless renewed is a policy that was built to be corrected.
  • Distinguish "I predict X" from "things could go wrong." Only the first is analysis. The second is a mood, and it can be used to defend anything.

The reason this subsection sits in the systems act is that second-order effects are not a quirk of human folly. They are the normal behaviour of a complex system receiving a signal: it responds, and the response is part of the outcome. What looks like a straight line from cause to effect almost always has a loop attached, and the loop is where the real story is.

Terminology

First-order effect — The direct, intended consequence of an intervention, in the targeted thing, on the timescale of the intervention itself.

Second-order effect — A consequence of the first-order effect, typically produced by the system adapting; arrives later and often elsewhere than the original action.

Third-order effect — A change in the rules, norms, or structure of a system caused by a second-order effect, which then constrains future options.

Unanticipated consequences (Merton) — The general phenomenon of purposive action producing outcomes outside the actor's intention; Merton's named sources include ignorance, error, immediacy of interest, and self-defeating prophecy.

Rebound effect — The increase in consumption of a resource caused by making it more efficient or cheaper, partially or wholly offsetting the intended saving.

Jevons paradox — The historical statement of rebound: improved efficiency in coal use increased total coal consumption rather than reducing it.

Induced demand — Additional usage generated by expanded capacity, classically in road networks, where new lanes fill with trips that were previously suppressed or routed elsewhere.

Risk compensation (Peltzman effect) — The tendency for people to take more risk when a safety measure reduces the perceived cost of risk; real in some domains, contested in others.

Perverse incentive — A reward structure that makes the undesirable behaviour the profitable one; the "cobra effect."

Displacement — The relocation of a problem rather than its reduction, often across a jurisdictional or measurement boundary.

Policy resistance — The property of a system in which actors work at cross purposes, so that interventions are absorbed and the system returns to its prior state.

Feedback delay — The lag between an action and its consequence returning to affect the actor; the main reason second-order effects escape the evaluation window.

Sunset clause — A provision that an intervention expires unless deliberately renewed, forcing periodic re-examination of its downstream effects.

Proportionality rule — Matching the depth of consequences analysis to the stakes, reversibility, and information available, rather than tracing causal chains indefinitely.
