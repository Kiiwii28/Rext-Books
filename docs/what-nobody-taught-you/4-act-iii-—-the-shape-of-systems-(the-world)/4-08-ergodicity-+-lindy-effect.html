4-08 Ergodicity + Lindy effect

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

\Two of the strangest results in this act are really one result wearing two costumes. Both say that time is not a neutral backdrop against which outcomes happen — it is a variable that changes what the outcomes mean. The first, ergodicity, asks whether the average across many people at a single moment equals the average experienced by one person across many moments. Very often it does not, and when it does not, expected value stops describing anybody's actual life. The second, the Lindy effect, asks what the age of a thing tells you about how much longer it will last. Both concern the gap between the ensemble and the trajectory, and both are ignored constantly, at a price.

The average that no one experiences

Ensemble time vs. time average

Statistics gives you two different kinds of average, and the fine print on which one applies is usually skipped.

  • The ensemble average takes a snapshot: many people, one moment, average across them.
  • The time average follows one person: one trajectory, many moments, what they actually accumulate.

A process is ergodic when the two coincide. Coin flips are ergodic: roll 10,000 people through one flip each, or one person through 10,000 flips, and the averages match. Betting your net worth on a coin is not ergodic, and the gap between the two averages is not a rounding error — it is the entire story.

The practical consequence is blunt: expected value is an ensemble concept. It is the right criterion for whoever holds the ensemble. It is the wrong criterion for whoever is one trajectory.

The coin flip that makes everyone rich and no one rich

Ole Peters' canonical example. You have $1,000 and the chance to flip a fair coin repeatedly. Heads: your wealth rises 50%. Tails: it falls 40%.

Compute the ensemble average return on a single flip: 0.5 × (+50%) + 0.5 × (−40%) = +5%. Positive expected value, comfortably so. A casino, an insurer, or an investment fund holding thousands of these positions grows about 5% per flip. If that were the whole analysis, you would bet everything, every time.

Now follow one person. Their wealth is multiplied by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails. Ten flips containing five heads and five tails multiply wealth by 1.5⁵ × 0.6⁵ = 0.9⁵ ≈ 0.59. Order does not matter — only the count. And since over 100 flips the number of heads is almost surely near 50, the typical trajectory lands near 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ $5. The median participant is down 99.5%. Meanwhile the ensemble average is 1.05¹⁰⁰ ≈ $131,500 — pulled up entirely by the vanishingly rare all-heads trajectory that essentially nobody occupies.

The average is real. It is also a description of a population you are not a member of.

Why multiplication breaks the intuition

The reason is arithmetic, not psychology. When gains and losses add — a salary, a subsidy — the average accumulates and the arithmetic mean is the right summary. When they multiply — a portfolio, a reputation, a body, a business — the ordering-free average is the geometric mean, and the geometric mean of 1.5 and 0.6 is √0.9 ≈ 0.949, a 5% loss per round. Same two numbers, opposite conclusions, depending on whether the process adds or multiplies.

This is also why a stock that gains 50% then loses 50% ends at 75% of where it started, despite an "average return" of zero. The gap between the arithmetic and geometric mean is the volatility drag, and it is the reason long-run wealth is described by log growth rather than by return.

Most things you actually care about are multiplicative: wealth, health, freedom, credibility, a functioning company. And multiplicative processes have a feature additive ones lack — a floor at zero that you cannot recover from.

Ruin is the whole game

An absorbing barrier is a state you cannot leave. Bankruptcy, imprisonment, death, a shattered reputation, a fallen company. In a repeated gamble, a small probability of ruin per round, however small, compounds against you: with ruin probability p per round, survival over n rounds is (1 − p)ⁿ, which tends toward zero. The only parameter that matters in the long run is whether the probability of ruin is zero.

Everything else is secondary. A strategy with a slightly worse average return and no possibility of ruin beats a strategy with a great average return and a 1-in-1,000 chance of annihilation, once you plan to repeat it more than a few hundred times. This is why "the expected value is positive" is the opening line of a bad decision rather than a defence of it.

What follows practically

  • Optimize for staying in the game. Ask of any repeated commitment: what is the worst case, and can I survive it and continue? If not, the size is wrong regardless of the expected return.
  • Bet fractions, not amounts. The Kelly criterion formalizes this: stake a fraction of your bankroll proportional to your edge, and never a fixed sum. The point is not to maximize this round; it is to maximize the growth rate of the trajectory.
  • Use logs for repeated processes. Average the logarithm of outcomes rather than the outcomes. Maximizing expected log wealth is the criterion that respects the time average.
  • Recognize which side of the ensemble you're on. An insurance company writing 100,000 policies is holding the ensemble and can reason in expectations. A single insured household is one trajectory, for whom the point of insurance is not expected value but the elimination of an absorbing barrier. Risk transfer is the act of buying someone else's ensemble average.
  • Diversify where possible. Independent trajectories multiply your chances of avoiding a shared ruin — with emphasis on independent.

The Lindy effect

The Lindy effect is the observation that, for a certain class of things, expected remaining life increases with current age. A book in print for 40 years will probably be in print in another 40. A technology that has survived a century of competition is likely to survive another. Benoît Mandelbrot named the effect after a New York delicatessen where the idea circulated; Nassim Taleb popularized it as a decision rule.

The underlying mathematics is unremarkable once you see it. If the lifetime of candidates in a domain follows a heavy-tailed distribution — a Pareto, say — then the conditional expected remaining life given survival to age t is proportional to t. Not constant, not decreasing: proportional. An item that has already lasted twice as long as another has, on average, twice as long still to go.

Why age is information

Lindy is not mysticism about tradition; it is a Bayesian argument about which class a thing belongs to. In any domain, most candidates die young. Survival past the early years is therefore evidence that you are looking at a member of the durable class, and the longer the survival, the stronger that evidence becomes. Age functions as a likelihood ratio about durability — a directly analogous move to the outside view, where you estimate from the record of comparable cases rather than from the vivid particulars of yours.

The domains where it bites hardest, because you usually have nothing better to go on:

  • Reading and learning. Choosing which book on a subject to invest months in.
  • Tools and standards. Which language, protocol, file format, or database to build on — where switching costs are high and the relevant horizon is decades.
  • Institutions and heuristics. Which procedures to trust when you cannot audit them yourself.
  • Ideas. Which concepts to bet your intellectual capital on.

Where Lindy does not apply

The distinction is perishable versus non-perishable. Lindy is a claim about things that do not wear out on their own, and it is simply false for things that do.

Two further limits matter more than the perishable caveat:

Persistence is not merit. Bloodletting survived roughly two millennia of medical practice. A convention can persist because of network lock-in rather than because it is good — which is exactly the path-dependence story of the previous chapter, and the reason Lindy is evidence about durability, not about quality or truth.

Environments can shift discontinuously. Lindy presumes the selection pressure that produced the survivor is still running. When the terrain changes — a new substrate, a regulatory break, a fat-tailed shock — the very robustness that made something durable can make it brittle and slow to adapt. Old does not mean safe forever; it means it has passed the tests the past actually administered.

Lindy versus survivorship bias

At first glance the two look like the same error: reading a graveyard and only seeing the headstones that are standing. They are related but not identical. Survivorship bias is a mistake about populations — inferring what makes a category succeed from a sample selected on success. Lindy is a claim about the future of an individual survivor, and under a heavy-tailed survival distribution it is a legitimate inference. The shared danger is one step further on: reading survival as approval. "It has lasted, therefore it is good" is survivorship bias wearing Lindy's coat.

Where the two ideas meet

They are two views of the same variable. Ergodicity tells you that you must survive to collect anything, and that the ensemble average is paid only to those who remain in the ensemble. Lindy tells you that survival is itself information about what tends to keep surviving.

Put together, they generate a usable disposition:

  1. Avoid absorbing barriers above all else. No average return is worth a nonzero chance of elimination from a process you intend to repeat.
  2. Prefer the old where the domain is stable and the thing is non-perishable, precisely because you lack the information to evaluate the new.
  3. Expect the old to be wrong about quality, and sometimes fatal about safety. A durable protocol can survive for reasons of lock-in, and a long-quiet system can be quietly accumulating a tail risk.
  4. When the environment has genuinely changed, Lindy's discount reverses — and you are in the fat-tailed territory of the next chapter, where the record of the past is least able to constrain the future.

The one-sentence version: survive first, and treat longevity as a prior rather than a verdict. Everything in this act about systems has assumed agents who remain in the system to observe it. These two ideas explain why some of them do not.

Terminology

Ergodicity — the property of a process in which the average across many independent individuals at one time equals the average experienced by one individual over time; when this fails, expectated value does not describe any single trajectory.

Non-ergodic process — a process where the ensemble average and the time average diverge, typically because outcomes multiply rather than add.

Ensemble average — the expected value computed across many parallel copies of a situation at a single moment; the statistic relevant to whoever holds a large pool of independent positions.

Time average — the value actually accumulated by one trajectory over many repetitions; the statistic relevant to an individual who cannot diversify across copies of themselves.

Geometric mean — the multiplicative equivalent of the average; the ordering-independent growth factor of a repeated multiplicative process, and the correct summary of long-run wealth.

Volatility drag — the shortfall between arithmetic average return and geometric growth caused by the dispersion of returns around the average.

Absorbing barrier — a state from which there is no exit, such as bankruptcy or death; the presence of any nonzero probability of reaching one dominates all other considerations in a repeated process.

Ruin — reaching an absorbing barrier; annihilation of the trajectory rather than a setback on it.

Kelly criterion — a betting rule that sizes stakes as a fraction of capital in proportion to the edge, chosen to maximize the expected growth rate of the trajectory rather than the payoff of a single round.

Expected log wealth — the objective function appropriate to repeated multiplicative decisions, because it optimizes the geometric growth rate and penalizes ruin.

Lindy effect — for non-perishable things in a stable environment, the expected remaining lifetime increases with current age; survival is evidence of membership in the durable class.

Non-perishable — a thing that does not wear out through use or time — ideas, books, languages, protocols, institutions — the class to which the Lindy effect applies.

Perishable — a thing with a rising hazard rate, such as a body or a machine part; for these, older means closer to failure, and Lindy does not apply.

Hazard rate — the probability of failing, given survival to this point; rising for perishables, falling for non-perishables.

Heavy-tailed survival distribution — a distribution such as the Pareto in which survival to a long age substantially raises the expected remaining lifetime.

Robustness versus merit — the distinction between something surviving because it is resistant to elimination and something surviving because it is good; lock-in produces the first without the second.
