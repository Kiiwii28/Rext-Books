4-01 Feedback loops (reinforcing-balancing)

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

A system's behaviour is rarely the sum of its parts' behaviour; it is generated by the way the parts feed back on one another. Feedback loops are the smallest unit of that structure — the grammar that turns a collection of things into a system with a personality of its own. Get the loops right and most of what follows in this act becomes readable at a glance: compounding, resistance, oscillation, thresholds, traps.

Two kinds of loop, and the naming trap

A feedback loop exists whenever a change in some quantity eventually produces a further change in that same quantity, through a chain of causes that comes back around. There are exactly two ways the return can work, and they could hardly be more different.

A reinforcing loop (also called positive feedback) returns a change in the same direction: more leads to more, less leads to less. Left unopposed, it compounds. Every genuinely exponential phenomenon in the world — compound interest, viral transmission, a bank run, a rumour, learning that makes further learning easier — is a reinforcing loop running without a brake.

A balancing loop (also called negative feedback) returns a change in the opposite direction: more triggers less, less triggers more. It steers the system toward some level and holds it there. Thermostats, predator–prey cycles, inventory restocking, and body temperature are all balancing loops.

The naming trap is worth defusing immediately. "Positive" and "negative" here are mathematical, not moral: they describe the sign of the return, not whether the outcome is desirable. A reinforcing loop can be a virtuous cycle or a death spiral, and a balancing loop can hold a system at a healthy set point or hold a failing institution exactly where it is. If you remember one thing from this subsection, remember that reinforcing means diverging, balancing means converging — nothing more.

Reading a loop: four conventions

Loops are usually drawn as a causal loop diagram, and four conventions make them readable.

  1. Nodes are variables, not events. "Customer count," "soil fertility," "trust" — quantities that can rise or fall. "The launch" is not a node.
  2. Arrows are causal influence, not sequence. A → B means that if A changes, B changes too, other things equal. Loop diagrams have no left-to-right reading order.
  3. Links carry a sign. A + link means the two move in the same direction; a − link means they move in opposite directions.
  4. Count the minus signs. Go around the loop: an even number of − links (including zero) makes it reinforcing, an odd number makes it balancing.

Reinforcing loops: growth is not a property, it is a process

We tend to talk about growth as though it were something an entity has, like mass. The loop view says otherwise: growth is what a reinforcing loop does when nothing stops it. That reframing pays off immediately, because it tells you where to look when you want to change the trajectory — at the loop, not at the entity.

Two consequences follow. First, because the change is proportional to the current level, reinforcing loops are slow at first and then startling: a doubling that takes a year becomes eight doublings in three. Human intuition tracks linear change and misjudges this badly, which is why the chapter on exponential intuition exists and why a reinforcing loop found early is worth more than one found late.

Second, and more useful in practice: reinforcing loops live in rules and incentives, not in physics. A firm that reinvests a fixed share of revenue has a reinforcing loop; the same firm with a policy of paying out everything does not. A team that documents what it learns gets a reinforcing competence loop; one that does not, gets a flat line. Loops are partly designed and partly inherited, which connects them directly to the question of emergent versus designed order.

Balancing loops: every one has a goal, and the goal may not be yours

A balancing loop can only balance against something. It always contains an implicit reference point — a set point, a target, a norm, a capacity limit. The thermostat compares and corrects; so does a market finding a price, a body regulating glucose, and an organisation reproducing its existing headcount.

This matters because pushing a system away from a balancing loop's goal does not simply work; it generates a counterforce that grows with the size of the push. Systems people call the result policy resistance, and it explains a specific and familiar frustration: the intervention that looked obviously correct, absorbed enormous effort, and changed nothing measurable. The loop was not broken. It was doing its job.

Crucially, you cannot read the goal off anyone's stated intentions. A department that resists every reorganisation is balancing against disruption, not against efficiency, and its goal is stability. The goal is inferred from behaviour — from what the system keeps returning to — and often it is set by structure nobody chose: a budget cycle, a reporting line, a performance metric. Where a measure is wired into a balancing loop, you get the failure discussed under Goodhart's Law: the loop protects the number rather than the thing the number was meant to track.

Delays: why balancing loops oscillate

A balancing loop with no delay settles smoothly. Add a delay and it overshoots, because the correction arrives after the information that triggered it has gone stale.

Everyone has met this loop in the shower: you turn the tap, nothing happens, you turn it further, then it scalds. The same structure produces ordering cycles in supply chains, boom-and-bust in hiring, overfishing, and the repeated pattern of aggressive antibiotic use followed by resistance and then newer, more expensive antibiotics. Delay converts a stabilising loop into an oscillating one, and a large enough delay converts it into something worse: correction that arrives so late it amplifies the original problem.

This is also the mechanism underneath the tipping points discussed elsewhere in this act. A threshold occurs when a reinforcing loop becomes strong enough to outrun the balancing loop that used to contain it — melting ice darkening and absorbing more heat, a bank whose losses make lenders pull funding and generate further losses. Thresholds are not mysterious. They are a change of which loop is winning.

Loop dominance: which loop is winning now

Real systems contain both loop types, and their behaviour over time is largely a story of dominance shifting. In the early life of a product, a reinforcing loop is unopposed and you see an exponential curve. As it consumes its market, balancing loops — saturation, competition for attention, support capacity — take over, and the curve flattens into the familiar S. If the balancing signals arrive late, the flattening becomes an overshoot and then a crash.

Forecasting the shape of a system's trajectory is far easier than forecasting the number — which is a useful discipline, because shape is what determines whether you should be building capacity or bracing for a crash, and it is genuinely testable.

Worked example: a subscription business

A small software company has two loops it never drew.

Reinforcing: customers → word of mouth → customers. Slow to start, then compounding.
Balancing: customers → load on support and onboarding → slower responses → cancellations → fewer customers.
The delay: churn data is reported monthly, so the balancing loop is invisible for roughly a quarter.

The intuitive response to flat growth is to push the reinforcing loop harder — spend on marketing, run a promotion. That works briefly, and then makes things worse, because the extra customers deepen the balancing loop while the churn signal is still three months stale. By the time cancellations show up in the dashboard, the cause is no longer visible. This is the archetype known as fixes that fail, and the fix is structural: address the constraint in the balancing loop (capacity, onboarding design, or reducing how much support customers need) rather than adding fuel to the loop that is already hungry.

Notice what the loop diagram bought you. It did not produce a number. It produced a prediction you could have written down in advance and checked — promotions will raise signups and lower retention within two quarters — which is exactly what the Adversary question in the MAPS audit asks for.

A short field guide

Archetype Structure Signature
Virtuous / vicious cycle One reinforcing loop Compounding up or down
Limits to growth Reinforcing + balancing S-curve, then plateau
Fixes that fail Symptom fix + side-effect loop Relief, then worse
Escalation Two reinforcing loops feeding each other Arms races (see Moloch)
Success to the successful Reinforcing loop allocating to whoever is ahead Winner-take-all (see path dependence and lock-in)
Shared-resource depletion Many agents, one capacity-limited balancing loop Overuse (see commons and Ostrom)

Most messy real-world situations are two or three of these stacked, which is why drawing them — six nodes, honest signs — beats arguing about them.

Using loops without becoming a mystic

"Everything is a feedback loop" explains nothing, and a loop diagram that cannot be wrong is decoration. Two habits keep the tool honest.

Ask the three questions. Is this process diverging or converging? What goal is the balancing loop actually holding, as revealed by behaviour rather than by what anyone says? Where are the delays, and how long? Those three answers usually predict the shape of what happens next.

Look for leverage in structure, not effort. Pushing harder on a variable inside a loop is the weakest intervention available. Faster sensing (shortening a delay) is stronger. Changing the links — adding a loop, breaking one, moving where the information arrives — is stronger still. Changing the loop's goal is strongest and hardest, which is why it is usually a political act rather than an analytical one.

And keep the discipline from Act II: a loop diagram is a map. Ask what it discards, and name in advance the observation that would tell you the loop is not the binding structure after all. That is what separates systems thinking from a vocabulary for sounding wise.

Terminology

Feedback loop — A chain of causation in which a change in a quantity eventually returns to change that same quantity.

Reinforcing loop (positive feedback) — A loop whose return acts in the same direction as the original change; produces compounding growth or collapse.

Balancing loop (negative feedback) — A loop whose return opposes the original change; steers the system toward a reference level.

Causal loop diagram — A drawing of variables and signed causal links used to identify loop structure; not a sequence or a process flow.

Link polarity (+ / −) — Whether two linked variables move in the same direction (+, even) or opposite directions (−, odd); an odd count of − links around a circuit makes it balancing.

Set point (reference level, goal) — The implicit target a balancing loop regulates toward, inferred from behaviour rather than from stated intentions.

Loop dominance — Which loop is currently determining a system's behaviour; dominance can shift over time, producing changes of regime.

Policy resistance — The tendency of a system to absorb interventions that push it away from its balancing loops' goals.

Delay — A lag between an action and its effect; in a balancing loop, the principal cause of oscillation and overshoot.

Overshoot — Correction that arrives after the deviation has grown, so the response exceeds what was needed.

S-curve — The characteristic trajectory of a reinforcing loop meeting a limit: exponential growth, inflection, plateau.

Leverage point — A place where a small structural change alters system behaviour; strongest at the level of links and goals, weakest at the level of parameters.

Archetype — A recurring loop configuration (limits to growth, fixes that fail, escalation, success to the successful, and others) with a recognisable signature.

Fixes that fail — An archetype in which a symptomatic fix triggers a side-effect loop that eventually restores or worsens the original problem.
