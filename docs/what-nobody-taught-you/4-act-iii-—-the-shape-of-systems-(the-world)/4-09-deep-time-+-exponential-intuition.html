4-09 Deep time + exponential intuition

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

Two of the most consequential facts about the world are invisible for the same reason: they sit outside the range of magnitudes the mind handles well. The first is how old the past is. The second is how fast a quantity growing at a constant percentage rate leaves everything else behind.

They are usually taught as separate topics — one from geology, one from finance. They belong together, because they are the same failure of imagination pointing in opposite directions. Deep time is the past scaled up until human history vanishes; exponential growth is the future scaled up until linear expectation vanishes. In both cases your intuition quietly substitutes something human-sized for the real quantity, and it does so without any sense of having made a substitution.

The past is bigger than you can feel

The Earth is about 4.54 billion years old. Numbers at that scale are unreadable — "billion" is a word, not a quantity. The standard fix is compression: shrink Earth's history into one calendar year, and see where things land.

Date (compressed year) Event Real time
Jan 1 Earth forms 4.54 bya
~Mar 8 First evidence of life ~3.7 bya
~Jun 21 Oxygen floods the atmosphere ~2.4 bya
~Aug 8 Complex cells appear ~1.8 bya
~Nov 18 Cambrian explosion ~538 mya
~Dec 26 Dinosaurs go extinct 66 mya
~11:25 p.m. Dec 31 Homo sapiens emerges ~300,000 ya
~11:58:36 p.m. Agriculture begins ~12,000 ya
~11:59:25 p.m. Writing is invented ~5,000 ya
~11:59:58 p.m. Industrial Revolution ~250 ya

All of recorded history occupies the final thirty-five seconds of the year. The industrial era — the fossil-fuel, population, and technology explosion that built the world you are sitting in — occupies the last two seconds.

If the calendar feels too abstract, walk it: taking one step per year, the extinction of the dinosaurs is about 66 million steps away, roughly 50,000 km, more than a lap around the Earth.

Why the intuition refuses

The mind anchors to what it can observe. Geological change is real, but it is slow enough that within a human lifetime a mountain looks like a noun rather than a verb. Before James Hutton and Charles Lyell, the dominant assumption was that the Earth's features had to be explained by a handful of catastrophes compressed into a short history — because no one could feel enough time for ordinary processes to do the work. Hutton, standing at Siccar Point in 1788 looking at a junction of two rock sequences laid down, tilted, and eroded, saw the arithmetic: ordinary rain and tide, applied for long enough, are enough. Uniformitarianism — present processes, given time, explain the past — is essentially deep time plus logic.

The correction did not come cheaply. Lord Kelvin calculated the Earth's age from cooling physics and got 20–40 million years, which was too short for evolution, and he was working from the best physics available. He was wrong because he did not know about radioactivity, which both heats the interior and provides the clock that eventually settled the question. Radiometric dating replaced inference from cooling with a direct measurement, and the number jumped by two orders of magnitude. That episode is worth remembering: a confidently derived, quantitatively precise answer can be wrong because of a missing mechanism, and only a better measurement catches it.

Where deep time bites

  • Evolution and biology. Species, immune systems, and ecosystems are the residue of time scales no one can picture. Their "design" is not design; it is iteration over more attempts than there have been seconds in recorded history.
  • Resources. Fossil fuels, aquifers, and mineral deposits form over millions of years and are consumed over decades. Nothing about the extraction rate is matched by the replenishment rate — a point that matters far more once you have the second half of this section.
  • Recovery times. After a mass extinction, biodiversity takes millions of years to rebuild. Damage and repair run on different clocks, and the clock you can see is the fast one.
  • Institutional design. The United States' nuclear waste repository at WIPP is required to warn people away for 10,000 years — roughly three times the length of recorded history. The Long Now Foundation builds a clock meant to run for ten millennia. Both projects exist because ordinary institutions, whose planning horizon is an electoral cycle, are not natively equipped for the timescales involved.
  • Path dependence. Deep time is what makes history load-bearing: choices made in a brief window can lock a system into a state that outlasts the people who made them.

The future can be faster than you can feel

Now run the failure in reverse. In linear growth, a quantity increases by a fixed amount per period: £2,000 a year, one new customer a month. In exponential growth, it increases by a fixed proportion of its current size: 7% a year, 30% a month. The two look identical at small scale and are qualitatively different at large scale.

The cleanest demonstration is a lily pond. A patch of lilies doubles in area every day, and it covers the whole pond on day 48.

  • Day 44: 0.4% covered. Nothing is happening. Nobody convenes a meeting.
  • Day 46: a quarter of the pond.
  • Day 47: half the pond — one day of slack remains, and the pond is still, visibly, only half full.
  • Day 48: full.

The last doubling does as much as all previous growth combined. That is not a quirk of pond lilies; it is arithmetic. A quantity that doubles has, at any moment, accumulated exactly as much in the most recent doubling as in everything before it.

The classical illustrations are all versions of this. The inventor who asks for one grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard and double on each subsequent square is owed about 1.8 × 10¹⁹ grains — roughly a year of global rice production, on the last square alone. A sheet of paper 0.1 mm thick, folded forty-two times, is about 440,000 km thick: past the Moon. During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, case counts doubled every two to three days in many countries; a fortnight of apparent calm was two or three doublings of hidden momentum.

Doubling time: the one number to carry

The rule of 72 converts a growth rate into a doubling time: doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ percentage growth rate per period. At 7% a year, something doubles every ~10 years. At 1% a year, every ~72 years. At 2% a month, every 3 years.

Worked example. Two debts. One charges 6% a year; one charges 24%. The first doubles in about 12 years; the second in about 3. Over a twenty-year horizon, the first has grown roughly 3.2× and the second roughly 78×. This is why the rate — not the balance, not the monthly payment — governs the endgame, and why arguing about the balance while ignoring the rate is arguing about the wrong variable.

Worked example. Global GDP has grown at roughly 2% a year since the industrial era. Seventy-two divided by two gives a doubling every ~36 years: about two doublings per human lifetime, roughly 20,000 doublings' worth of nothing special in a single century, and — this is the point — a completely impossible factor if extended across deep time. At 1% a year for 10,000 years, the multiplier is about 10⁴³. There is no such quantity of anything. Exponential growth is always a phase, never a permanent regime.

Why the intuition refuses, again

  • We extrapolate additively. "It grew by three last year, so it will grow by three next year." The default forecast is a straight line, and it is wrong in exactly one direction.
  • The early phase looks flat. A system eight doublings from its limit is at 0.4% of it. Nothing observable distinguishes that from a system that is not growing at all.
  • Magnitude sense is logarithmic. Weber–Fechner compression means that a constant ratio feels like a constant distance. On a logarithmic axis — where exponentials are straight lines — everything looks like a gentle slope. Our mental scaling is the log chart, while our forecasting is the linear one.
  • We conflate rate with amount. A large base growing slowly produces huge absolute increases; a small base growing fast produces trivial ones. Both "3% growth" and "300% growth" describe systems that can be small or enormous next year.
  • The late phase looks like a change in kind. Commentators describe it as disruption, a new era, a singularity — when it is the same process that was running, unnoticed, in the days of the half-empty pond.

The bend

Nothing grows exponentially forever, because the pond is finite. Growth compresses into an S-curve: slow, steep, then a plateau as the constraint binds. Human population is the standard example — about 1 billion in 1804, 2 billion in 1927, 4 billion in 1974, 8 billion in 2022, with the doubling time stretching toward a century and the plateau in sight.

The interesting question at any point on the curve is never "how fast is it growing?" but "what is the constraint, and how far away is it in doublings?" Eight doublings is 0.4% of the limit and looks like nothing. Two is half. That is the margin you actually have.

Where the two ideas meet

Put the compressed timeline and the doubling time on the same chart and you get the modern world. Ten thousand years of essentially flat human impact, then a vertical line somewhere in the last two seconds of the planetary year. The Great Acceleration — population, energy use, materials extraction, atmospheric carbon, extinction rate, all bending upward together after roughly 1950 — is what an exponential looks like when it is plotted against deep time.

The reverse pressure runs too. Fast exponential systems push on reservoirs that operate on deep-time clocks, and the clocks do not speed up to match.

Atmospheric carbon persists for centuries to millennia. Groundwater drawn down from aquifers recharges over centuries or not at all. Extinction is permanent on any human timescale. Soil loss is measured in decades and soil formation in centuries. The asymmetry is the whole point: the fast variable moves the slow one in years, and the slow one then governs the fast one for a very long time.

There is one more mirror image worth naming, and it is handled elsewhere in this book: the same exponential appears in discounting, where a 7% discount rate makes a century from now worth about a tenth of a percent of today — the far future made invisible by a mechanism no one ever chose deliberately.

Calibrating both

  • Convert to doublings. Ask how many doublings to the limit, not how many years. Half the distance to any ceiling is one doubling.
  • Count doublings, not percentages. Four doublings is 16×; ten is 1,000×. Percentages hide this; doublings do not.
  • Ask what the constraint is. If you cannot name it, you have not understood the system — and the S-curve will find you.
  • Draw it to scale, on both axes. A linear chart hides early growth; a log chart hides the approach to the ceiling. Neither alone tells the truth.
  • Physicalize deep time. Steps, calendar years, a strip of paper. The failure is not arithmetic, it is scale, and scale responds to embodiment.
  • Distrust flat stretches. A 0.4% lily pond and a 0% lily pond are indistinguishable until two days before the end.

Terminology

Deep time — The geological timescale of hundreds of millions to billions of years, against which human history is a vanishingly small interval.

Uniformitarianism (Hutton, Lyell) — The principle that the same ordinary processes observable now, given enough time, explain geological features; deep time converted into an explanatory method.

Compressed timeline — A representation of a vast interval as a familiar one (a calendar year, a single step-per-year walk) so the ratios become perceptible.

Exponential growth — Growth by a constant proportion of the current quantity per period; produces a doubling every fixed interval rather than a fixed increment.

Linear growth — Growth by a constant absolute amount per period; the mind's default forecast, and the source of the systematic underestimate of exponential processes.

Doubling time — The interval in which an exponentially growing quantity doubles; the natural unit for reasoning about exponential processes.

Rule of 72 — The approximation that doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ growth rate per period.

Logarithmic scale — A scale on which equal distances represent equal ratios; renders exponentials as straight lines, which is why they look harmless.

S-curve (logistic growth) — The characteristic shape of growth that begins exponentially, steepens, then levels off as a constraint binds.

Overshoot — Growth that passes a system's sustainable ceiling before declining, typically producing a sharper fall than the rise.

Constraint / carrying capacity — The limit that eventually bends an exponential; the thing to name when you estimate how many doublings remain.

Great Acceleration — The post-1950 synchronised upturn in population, energy, materials, and environmental impact; an exponential plotted against deep time.

Deep-time reservoir — A stock (carbon, soil, groundwater, biodiversity) that accumulates over geological time and is depleted, often irreversibly on human timescales, in decades.

Temporal asymmetry — The pattern in which damage to a slow reservoir happens on a fast clock while recovery happens on a slow one.
