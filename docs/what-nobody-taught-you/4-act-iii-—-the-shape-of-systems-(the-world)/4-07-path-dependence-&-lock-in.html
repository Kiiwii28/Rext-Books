4-07 Path dependence & lock-in

Part of 4 Act III — The Shape of Systems (the world)

Path dependence is the claim that where you end up depends on where you happened to start and in what order things happened along the way — that history is not just decor, but structure. Lock-in is its strongest form: a state you would not choose from scratch, would not freely enter today, and cannot easily leave, because the cost of leaving is produced by the fact that everyone else is inside. Together they explain why so many things in the world — keyboards, rail gauges, electrical voltages, legal systems, your career — look less like the best available answer and more like the oldest surviving one.

Two kinds of "history matters"

Almost everything is historically dependent in a trivial sense. A scarred table is different because of what happened to it. That is state dependence or hysteresis: the present state depends on the past path, but there is nothing puzzling about it and no alternative to recover.

Path dependence in the sense that matters is narrower and stronger. It requires three things:

  1. Multiple equilibria were available. At some point, more than one stable outcome was reachable.
  2. The selection among them was contingent. Which one won was decided by early events that were, at the time, small, arbitrary, or lucky — not by a decisive quality difference.
  3. The outcome is now sticky. The losing options are not merely unchosen; they have become progressively harder to choose. Reversing would cost more today than it would have yesterday.

The diagnostic question is therefore not "did history matter?" but this: if we could start over with today's knowledge and today's costs of switching erased, would we choose this again? If the answer is yes, you are looking at a settled standard. If the answer is no, but we are never going to change it, you are looking at lock-in.

The distinction is worth holding on to, because invoking "lock-in" as a synonym for "things persist" turns a sharp analytical tool into a shrug.

The engine: increasing returns to adoption

Ordinary goods obey diminishing returns — as more people use something, it gets more crowded and less attractive, which pulls toward the best option. Lock-in arises where the opposite holds. When returns increase with adoption, success is self-feeding: the front-runner gets stronger precisely because it is the front-runner.

Mechanism What it means Example
Learning by doing More users → more production → cheaper and better, along the learning curve Semiconductors, solar panels, aircraft
Network effects Each additional user makes the good more valuable to every existing user Telephones, social platforms, payment rails
Scale economies in complements A big installed base attracts a big supply of supporting goods and skills Software, spare parts, trained mechanics
Adaptive expectations People adopt what they expect others to adopt; the expectation is self-fulfilling Betting on the "obvious" winner
Sunk investment in durable capital Physical and human capital bought for one standard cannot be repurposed cheaply Track, charging plugs, credentials

The canonical formal model is the Pólya urn. Start with one red and one blue ball. Draw at random, and put the drawn ball plus another of the same colour back in the urn. Early draws are pure chance, but each one biases all subsequent draws toward its own colour. Run it a hundred times and you get a distribution of heavily one-sided outcomes, decided mostly by the first few picks. That is increasing returns to adoption in miniature: the process is random, the result is not.

Note what this does to the idea of the market outcome. Where returns increase, there is no single equilibrium an efficient market settles on; there are several, and history picks among them.

The critical juncture

Lock-in is not a permanent condition of the world; it is what happens after a window closes. The window is the critical juncture: a period when the structure was fluid, alternatives were live, and a small push could have sent things down a different branch.

Three features of critical junctures matter in practice.

  • The margin can be tiny. A 51–49 split at the moment a standard crystallises is often enough to determine the next century. Nothing about a one-point lead reflects a one-point quality difference.
  • They are usually only visible in hindsight. While the window is open, the choice looks like a normal procurement decision. Afterwards, it looks like destiny.
  • They cluster around shocks. Wars, depressions, pandemics, regulatory resets, and technological discontinuities reopen windows that had been welded shut. This is why institutional change so often looks like punctuated equilibrium: long periods of stasis, then a rapid burst that resets the path for decades.

Worked example: QWERTY, and how much of the story is true

The standard telling: Christopher Latham Sholes's 1870s typewriter layout deliberately scattered common letter pairs to slow typists and stop the mechanical arms from jamming. When jamming became obsolete, a demonstrably faster layout (August Dvorak's, patented 1936) was available — yet QWERTY survived, locked in by typist training, manufacturer investment, and habit. Paul David made this the founding case study of path dependence in 1985; Brian Arthur generalised the economics; and the term entered the general vocabulary.

The standard telling is also contested, and the contest is more instructive than the story. In 1990, Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis argued that the anti-jamming origin is dubious (the layout's quirks may owe more to telegraph operators and to the sales demo word "TYPEWRITER" sitting on the top row), and that the evidence for Dvorak's superiority rests on studies that were small, methodologically weak, and partly conducted by Dvorak himself. Their conclusion: QWERTY may simply be adequate, and the switching cost may simply exceed the gain.

Two lessons survive the dispute intact. First, "locked in" and "locked in to something worse" are different claims, and only the second one is an indictment. Lots of durable standards are mediocre but fine, and the correct move is to leave them alone. Second, the honest version of the case is about irreversibility, not inefficiency. Whether or not Dvorak is better, retraining a hundred million typists is not worth a few percent of typing speed — and that is the real content of lock-in: a barrier, not necessarily a tragedy.

Getting out: how lock-ins break

Lock-ins do break, and they break in recognisable ways. Almost all of them work by attacking the self-reinforcing loop rather than the incumbent's quality.

Escape route How it works Example
Coordinated simultaneous switch Everyone moves on the same day, so no one bears the transition alone Sweden's Dagen H, 3 September 1967: the entire country switched from driving on the left to driving on the right at 5 a.m.
Mandate or regulation An authority internalises the coordination problem and forces the change Metrication; the EU's common charging-port mandate
Adapter layer / modularity Decouple the locked-in layer so innovation happens above or below it The layered internet: TCP/IP changed everything without replacing the copper
Attack from an adjacent segment Enter where the incumbent's network and installed base do not matter Mobile phones versus landlines in countries with no landline infrastructure
Technology discontinuity A new substrate resets the installed base to zero Streaming versus physical media

The most striking historical case is rail gauge. The American South standardised on 5-foot track while the North used 4 feet 8½ inches; by the 1880s the mismatch meant that through-freight had to be unloaded and reloaded at the boundary. The fix, in 1886, was a coordinated one-day narrowing of roughly 11,500 miles of southern track, with the final half inch shaved off line by line over the following years. Hundreds of independent railroads, each with every incentive to free-ride on the others' conversion, did it simultaneously because the cost of not coordinating had become intolerable.

That is the general shape of escape: not a better argument, but a change in the payoff structure — usually a shock, a mandate, or a bridge technology that makes the switch cheap enough for everyone at once.

Where it bites

  • Technology and standards. Which protocols, file formats, and platforms your organisation bet on determines what it can build for a decade.
  • Institutions. Constitutions, legal doctrines, property regimes, and tax codes are path-dependent artefacts; the fact that a rule exists tells you more about 1789 or 1945 than about today (this is where path dependence meets the legibility and institutions material).
  • Careers and skills. Human capital is the least liquid asset you own. A decade in one specialty is both expertise and an exit barrier.
  • Cities and infrastructure. Street grids and transit routes outlive the reasons they were built.
  • Your own commitments. The sunk cost fallacy is lock-in at the scale of one person: you continue because you have already paid, which is exactly backwards, since the money is gone either way.

Using it

Four habits follow from taking path dependence seriously.

Ask what is reversible. Decisions are not all the same kind. Some are two-way doors you can walk back through cheaply; others are one-way doors that will define your option set for years. Spend deliberation proportionally to reversibility, not to how important a decision feels.

Look for the switching cost, and ask who pays it. Lock-in that costs a cent per person and benefits a million people is not a problem. Lock-in where the cost is concentrated on a few and the benefit is diffuse is precisely when coordination fails and mandates become justified.

Prefer keeping a live exit. Modular designs, open standards, portable data, and skills that transfer are all forms of optionality, and optionality is what you are buying when you pay slightly more for the interoperable option.

Ignore "would you choose it again?" at your peril — and do not over-obey it. The counterfactual test is how you detect lock-in, not an argument for dismantling it. If the cost of escape exceeds the gain, the honest answer is to keep the standard and stop calling it a mistake.

There is a quiet implication here that connects back to the earliest threads of this book. Lock-in is what it feels like to live downstream of decisions you did not make and cannot see. The routes through your city, the letters on your keyboard, the calendar you keep, and the credentials employers demand were all contingent resolutions of old critical junctures — and they will be resolved again, at the next window, by whoever happens to be paying attention when it opens.

Terminology

Path dependence — The property of a process in which the outcome depends on the sequence of events that produced it, not merely on current conditions; strong versions require multiple possible equilibria and contingent selection among them.

Lock-in — A state in which an option remains dominant because switching costs, generated largely by the option's own adoption, make exit progressively harder.

Increasing returns to adoption — The condition in which a technology, standard, or institution becomes more valuable or cheaper as more users adopt it, creating self-reinforcing momentum.

Multiple equilibria — The existence of more than one stable outcome a system could settle into; the precondition for path dependence to matter.

Contingency — The dependence of the outcome on early events that were small, arbitrary, or lucky rather than on decisive differences in merit.

Critical juncture — A period of fluidity in which alternatives are genuinely live and small events can determine which one becomes locked in.

Punctuated equilibrium — A pattern of long institutional or technological stability interrupted by brief bursts of rapid, path-resetting change.

Hysteresis / state dependence — Dependence of the present state on past events generally; weaker than path dependence and not necessarily a puzzle.

Switching cost — Everything required to move from one standard to another: retraining, replacement capital, lost network, coordination with others, risk.

Pólya urn — A model of increasing returns in which each random draw increases the future probability of drawing the same colour; shows how a random process can produce a strongly deterministic-looking result.

Sunk cost fallacy / escalation of commitment — Continuing an investment because of what has already been spent; lock-in operating within a single decision-maker.

Network effect — The increase in a good's value to each user as the number of users grows.

Two-way door / one-way door — A decision that is cheap to reverse versus one that is expensive or impossible to reverse; the practical test for how much deliberation a choice deserves.

Optionality — The value of holding open alternatives that can be exercised later; the direct antidote to premature lock-in.

Standard — A shared specification that lets independent parties interoperate; the beneficial face of the same increasing-returns dynamics that produce lock-in.
