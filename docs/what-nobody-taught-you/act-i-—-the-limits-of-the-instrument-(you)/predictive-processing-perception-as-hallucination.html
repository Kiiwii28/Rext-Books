Predictive processing - perception as hallucination

Part of Act I — The Limits of the Instrument (you)

The Brain as a Prediction Machine

Perception feels like reception: light hits your retina, sound hits your ears, and the world is delivered to you like a photograph. Predictive processing says the traffic runs mostly the other way. Your brain constantly generates a top-down guess about what is out there — a model of the causes of its sensory input — and compares that guess against the signal coming up from the senses. What propagates upward is not the image itself but the prediction error: the part of the input the guess failed to account for. Perception is the guess, corrected.

The striking implication is that ordinary perception and hallucination sit on the same continuum. Both are internally generated constructions; the difference is that perception is controlled by sensory input, while hallucination is not. You are, in Anil Seth's phrase, hallucinating all the time — it is just that when we agree about our hallucinations, we call it reality. Illusions are the leak in the system: the hollow-mask effect, the McGurk effect, and a shadowed surface that still looks white all reveal the prior winning against the evidence, because the brain has learned that its priors are usually the better bet.

Two practical consequences follow. First, perception is inference — the same logic as Bayesian reasoning, with priors standing in for base rates — so it inherits the same failure modes. Strong priors make you fast and efficient most of the time and blind precisely when the world has changed. Second, if the brain builds experience from a model, then memory, imagination, and perception are drawing on the same machinery — which is why remembering is reconstruction and why you can confabulate a reason for an action you never chose. The instrument does not report the world; it renders one.

Where this bites

  • Change blindness and inattentional blindness — you perceive a stable scene, but the model is filled in from expectation, not sampled continuously.
  • Expectation effects — a wine labeled expensive tastes better; the prior is doing part of the tasting.
  • Stereotyping and first impressions — priors about people operate exactly like priors about objects, and they resist disconfirming evidence for the same reason.
  • Cross-cultural and expertise differences — different priors, different percepts from identical input.

Terminology

Predictive processing — The theory that the brain continuously generates top-down predictions of sensory input and updates them using the resulting prediction errors.

Top-down prediction — An internally generated expectation about the causes of incoming sensory data, derived from prior experience.

Prediction error — The mismatch between what the brain predicted and what the senses actually delivered; the main signal traveling upward in the hierarchy.

Prior — A pre-existing belief or expectation that biases interpretation of new evidence, in perception as in Bayesian reasoning.

Controlled hallucination — Perception understood as a hallucination that is continuously constrained and corrected by sensory input, as opposed to an uncontrolled one.

Perceptual inference — The process of inferring the most likely external cause of sensory signals, analogous to Bayesian belief updating.

Percept — The experienced result of the prediction-and-correction process; what you take to be the world.
