Epistemic humility + calibrated confidence

Part of Act I — The Limits of the Instrument (you)

Epistemic humility is often confused with low confidence. It isn't. Humility is a claim about your process: that your beliefs are fallible, that your evidence is partial, and that you should be willing to revise. Confidence is a claim about a specific proposition: how likely it is to be true given what you now know. A person can be maximally humble and still say "I'm 97% sure" — provided they're right about 97% of the time they say it.

That property has a name: calibration. A forecaster is calibrated when the events assigned probability p occur about p of the time. Being calibrated is not the same as being accurate — you can be well calibrated and ignorant (always saying 50/50), or badly calibrated and often right in a lucky, overconfident way. Calibration is the easier half to fix and the half most people never measure, because it requires keeping score. The practical discipline is unglamorous: state probabilities numerically, write them down, resolve them later, and compare. Vague verbal hedges ("I think," "probably") cannot be scored, and so cannot be improved — which is exactly why they're so comfortable. This is where epistemic humility plugs into the Bayesian machinery: a probability is a bet, and a bet you never settle teaches you nothing.

The failure mode is asymmetric. Humans are, in aggregate, overconfident about hard questions and underconfident about easy ones — and the confidence gap widens as the topic gets more abstract and more socially rewarded to have an opinion on. Two habits correct for it. First, the outside view: before estimating anything, ask how often this class of thing happens, rather than reasoning forward from your own case (base rates beat narratives). Second, the reversal test: name in advance what evidence would change your mind. If nothing could, you're not holding a belief — you're holding an identity.

Humility and calibration are therefore two halves of one skill: humility keeps you updating, calibration tells you how much to update. Neither is a personality trait; both are scores you can raise. The uncomfortable implication is that most of the confidence you display at work, in argument, and in your own head has never been audited — not because you're dishonest, but because you've never had a mechanism that returns the answer.

Terminology

  • Epistemic humility — the stance that one's beliefs are fallible and evidence is partial; a claim about process, not a low probability estimate.
  • Calibrated confidence — confidence that matches reality: events assigned probability p occur roughly p of the time.
  • Overconfidence / underconfidence — systematic mismatch in either direction between stated and realised accuracy; typically overconfident on hard items, underconfident on easy ones.
  • Scoring rule (e.g. Brier score) — a numeric penalty for probabilistic forecasts that rewards stating your true probability rather than hedging; makes calibration measurable.
  • Outside view — estimating from the base rate of a reference class rather than from the specifics of your own case.
  • Reversal test — the practice of naming, in advance, the evidence that would change your mind; a belief that survives no possible evidence is not a belief but an identity.
