3-7 Epistemology of disagreement (when to defer)

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Most treatments of disagreement frame it as a contest: someone is wrong, and the job is to work out who. But once you accept that your beliefs are fallible and that you cannot audit them alone, disagreement changes role — it becomes an instrument, one of the few error-correction mechanisms a single mind has access to. The interesting question is no longer "who is right?" but "what does this disagreement license me to do with my confidence?" Answer that, and you can be wrong in public without being destabilised, and right in private without becoming a crank.

Three kinds of disagreement — only one is hard

Sorting matters, because the correct response differs sharply by type:

  • Disagreement from below. The other person has less evidence or less competence than you. No revision is owed on the object level — though they are often right about a local detail you've abstracted away, which is worth harvesting.
  • Disagreement from above. They have more evidence, better tools, or a track record you don't. The default is to defer — but deference has an identification problem (below).
  • Peer disagreement. Someone with the same evidence, the same competence, and the same honesty reaches the opposite conclusion. This is the hard case, and the one the philosophical literature is actually about.

The definition of an epistemic peer is deliberately strict: same evidence, same reasoning ability, same intellectual honesty, no distorting incentives. Most people you argue with fail one of those conditions, which is why "peer disagreement" is rarer than ordinary disagreement and why the intuition that you should always split the difference is wrong so much of the time.

Why peer disagreement is genuinely puzzling

Two positions define the debate.

Conciliationism (Elga, Christensen, Feldman) says that the bare fact of a peer's disagreement is higher-order evidence about your own reliability. I might be unable to find my error — that is exactly what an error looks like from the inside — but the fact that someone equally well placed reached a different answer is evidence that I've mis-stepped somewhere. On this view, you should move toward their position, often roughly to the midpoint.

Steadfastness (Kelly's "right reasons" view) replies that what matters is the first-order evidence. If I have correctly evaluated it, the disagreement is fully explained by their error, and the disagreement itself gives me no new reason to move. Concentrating on who did the evidence better is the only rational response.

Both capture something real. Conciliationism explains why persistent disagreement between competent people is humbling; the right-reasons view explains why a physicist should not halve their confidence in Newtonian mechanics because a neighbour disagrees. The practical resolution is that the tie is broken by asymmetry: not total symmetry, but asymmetry you can actually see.

What actually licenses deference

Actual peerhood is rare, so the operative skill is detecting the asymmetry. The recurring asymmetries:

Evidence access. If they have seen something you haven't, the disagreement is a pointer to evidence, not a verdict. Ask what they've seen.

Track record, not confidence. Deference is owed to demonstrated calibration, not to conviction. Someone whose 80% forecasts come true 80% of the time has earned weight; someone who is merely certain has not. Note that confidence and calibration come apart in the wrong direction — the loudest disputant is frequently the least scored. This is the payoff of keeping score at all.

Predictability of error. Experts fail in patterned ways, and patterns are usable. You do not need to master a field to notice that its practitioners are systematically optimistic about timelines, or systematically captured by whoever funds them.

Legibility. Can they state what evidence would change their mind? Someone who answers that question concretely is offering you a testable position; someone who cannot is offering you an identity.

Meta-expertise and its limits. You often cannot evaluate the content of an expert claim without already being an expert, so you fall back on proxies: credentials, the endorsement of other experts, track record, and incentives. This is rational but fragile — every proxy is a measure, and measures under pressure get gamed (which is why credentialism decays, and why "consensus" is worth less when careers depend on it). The workaround is to use several proxies and to weight the ones that are hard to fake: resolved predictions, records of admitting error, and being willing to lose arguments in public.

Count sources, not people

A crucial asymmetry runs the other way: apparent crowds are often one witness wearing many hats. The Condorcet jury theorem says a majority of independent voters, each better than chance, converges on the truth — but its independence assumption does the work, and real agreement is usually correlated. Five colleagues agreeing often means four of them read the fifth.

The diversity prediction theorem makes the same point arithmetically: crowd error equals average individual error minus diversity of the group. Correlated agreement adds nothing to the first term and subtracts from the second. This is why deference has a hidden cost — if everyone defers to the same apparent authority, the aggregate signal gets worse, and the disagreeing person who holds an independent line is performing a public service even when wrong.

When to hold firm

Holding firm is legitimate in specific, nameable situations — and illegitimate as a general mood.

  • The dispute is downstream of values. Expertise bears on what will happen, not on what you want. Defer on the forecast, keep the decision. (Facts and preferences are different kinds of claim; treating a technical answer as a value answer smuggles in an ought.)
  • Your evidence is genuinely private and unshareable. You were there; they weren't. The corollary is uncomfortable: you may be misremembering, which is why contemporaneous notes outrank retrospective certainty.
  • Their position cannot be falsified by anything. A claim that survives every possible observation is not a strong belief but a non-empirical one — you owe it no evidential deference.
  • Incentives predict the conclusion. Not always, but often enough to weight.

The failure mode here is motivated reasoning wearing the mask of principle. The test is the one you've already met: name what would change your mind. If nothing would, you're not defending a belief, you're defending a self.

A worked example

You estimate a project will take three months. A colleague with the same specifications estimates nine. Neither of you has privileged information.

Running the protocol: you are, on the formal definition, peers. You ask whether the disagreement is one claim or several — and it turns out to be several: you differ on how much rework testing will trigger, and on whether a dependency will slip. Localising it converts an argument about a number into two answerable questions. You then check track records and the reference class: how long do projects of this type usually take, and how accurate have your past estimates been? If the answer to the second is "I say three months, it takes five," your estimate is not a peer of theirs; it is a biased instrument, and you should move most of the way toward the better-calibrated estimator — not to the midpoint, and not to nine months either. If both of you are equally well calibrated, the honest number is somewhere in between, and the way to shrink the range is to go find evidence about rework and the dependency, not to argue about the number.

Running a disagreement

  1. Classify it: below, above, or genuine peer.
  2. Locate the crux — the smallest sub-claim on which your disagreement turns.
  3. Test for independence: is your evidence a second source or the same source twice?
  4. Compare calibration, not confidence.
  5. State what evidence would move you, and ask them to do the same.
  6. Defer on the object level if they're better placed; keep the meta-level judgement that they might be wrong.

The discomfort of this protocol is that it is sometimes an argument for submission: occasionally the right answer is that you should simply believe the person who knows more, and stop adjudicating. That is not intellectual weakness. It is what epistemic humility looks like when it has a target.

Terminology

  • Epistemic peer — someone with the same evidence, comparable competence, and comparable honesty on the question at hand.
  • Peer disagreement — a conflict of opinion between epistemic peers; the case where the rational response is genuinely contested.
  • Higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of your own reasoning, such as the fact that a competent person disagrees, rather than evidence about the subject itself.
  • Conciliationism — the view that peer disagreement provides higher-order evidence and should move you toward the other position.
  • Steadfastness / right-reasons view — the view that if you evaluated the first-order evidence correctly, the mere fact of disagreement gives you no reason to revise.
  • Asymmetry — a detectable difference between disputants in evidence access, calibration, or vulnerability to error; the practical tie-breaker over full symmetry.
  • Crux — the smallest specific sub-claim on which a disagreement turns; localising it converts an intractable dispute into answerable questions.
  • Independence of evidence — the property of two sources being error-correlated or not; only independent sources multiply the weight of agreement.
  • Diversity prediction theorem — crowd error equals average individual error minus group diversity; correlated agreement destroys the crowd's advantage.
  • Meta-expertise — knowledge about which experts to trust, used when you cannot evaluate the substance of a claim directly.
