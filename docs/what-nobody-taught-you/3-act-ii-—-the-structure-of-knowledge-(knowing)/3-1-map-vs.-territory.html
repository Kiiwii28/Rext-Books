3-1 Map vs. territory

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Alfred Korzybski's line — "the map is not the territory" — is usually quoted as a warning, but it begins as a statement of necessity. You cannot reason about the world directly; the world is too large, too fast, and too detailed. Every act of thought involves substituting something smaller and more tractable for the thing itself: a spreadsheet for a business, a résumé for a person, a spreadsheet's revenue column for a product. A map in this broad sense is any model, metric, category, story, or diagram that stands in for something else. The useful question is never "is this map true?" but "true of what, at what resolution, and for whose purpose?"

That reframing matters because it removes a false choice. Maps are not regrettable approximations of reality that we would abandon if we were smarter; they are the only way anything gets done. The territory has no labels, no units, and no boundaries — it just is. Drawing a boundary is itself a modelling act.

Why compression is the whole point

A map works by discarding. The London Underground map, redrawn by Harry Beck in 1933, does not show distances, curves, streets, or the Thames in its true shape. That is not sloppiness; it is the design. Beck's insight was that a passenger needs topology — which stations connect to which, and in what order — and that routing out the geography makes the diagram far easier to use for the one task it serves. A geographically accurate version would be worse at being a tube map.

The virtue of any model is that it can be run cheaply and repeatedly where the territory cannot: crash-test dummies and wind tunnels before real cars and real wings; A/B tests before national rollouts; a Monte Carlo simulation before a factory build. The compression is what makes iteration affordable. So the analysis of any model splits into two questions: what does it preserve (usually chosen deliberately), and what does it discard (usually chosen by default, and rarely examined)? The discarded half is where the trouble lives.

Three ways maps mislead

Omission. Every boundary is drawn where the territory has no line. National borders, fiscal years, org charts, product categories, and even biological species are administrative cuts through continua. The trouble comes when the cut is forgotten and the territory is assumed to be partitioned that way — when "Q4 performance" or "my department's headcount" is treated as a natural unit rather than a convenience.

Distortion. Flattening a sphere onto paper forces a projection, and every projection distorts something. The same is true of units and scales: linear axes hide exponential growth; averages hide distributions (a "mean waiting time" of nine minutes can coexist with a 40-minute tail); percentages conceal the base they are percentages of. Distortion is most dangerous when the grammar of the map becomes invisible — when a metric feels like a reading rather than a rendering.

Reification. The subtlest failure is treating the map as the thing it describes. This is the move that turns a measure into a target, at which point the measure degrades in a well-documented way; turns a résumé into a person, which is how survivorship-flavoured hiring happens; and turns an economic aggregate into "the economy," which is how a country can look healthier while its households do not.

Worked example: GDP as a territory-shaped hole

Gross domestic product is a superb map of one thing — the market value of final goods and services produced in a period — and a poor guide to nearly everything else. It counts a forest felled as a gain and the resulting flood as a further gain (rebuilding is production). It omits unpaid household work, so a society can "shrink" when people start cooking instead of buying meals. A hurricane raises it. And because it is an aggregate, it says nothing about who received what: a country's GDP can rise while median income falls.

None of this is an argument against measuring output. It is an argument against asking the map a question it was never drawn to answer. The failure is not in GDP — a good projection for its purpose — but in the user who forgets the projection is there. When a map is used for a purpose outside its drawing, the omission, distortion, and reification failures arrive together.

Keeping maps honest

Four habits separate people who use models from people who are used by them.

  • Ask what was discarded, and why. For any metric or model, name two things it cannot see. If you cannot, you have not understood it yet.
  • Inspect the projection. What are the units, the scale, the reference class, the time window? An exponential drawn on a linear axis is not a fact about the world; it is a fact about the drawing.
  • Go to the territory cheaply and often. Read the support tickets, visit the store, talk to the person behind the number. Direct contact is expensive per unit but is the only mechanism that corrects a model rather than reshuffling it.
  • Watch for closed loops. A model updated only by other models — theory by theory, dashboard by dashboard, second-hand summaries of other summaries — drifts without ever contradicting itself. The alarm sign is a model that has grown more confident while becoming less connected to any measurement of the thing it claims to describe. Redundancy helps only if the sources are genuinely independent; three estimates derived from one dataset are one estimate.

The portable test, borrowed from the discipline of calibration, is to say in advance where the map would be flatly wrong. "This hiring rubric fails for career-changers." "This forecast breaks if interest rates move two points." If no observation could show the map wrong, it has stopped being a model of the territory and become a piece of the organisation's identity — and it will be defended like one.

Terminology

  • Map (model) — any representation that stands in for a territory; includes metrics, categories, diagrams, narratives, and formal models.
  • Territory — the thing itself, in its full detail; never directly available to reasoning, only to observation.
  • Compression — the deliberate discarding of detail that makes a map usable; the source of both a model's power and its characteristic failures.
  • Projection — the distortion introduced when a complex reality is flattened into a representation; in practice, the units, scale, and framing that shape what the map can show.
  • Reification — treating the representation as the thing it represents; the error behind every measure that becomes a target.
  • Map–territory error — any inference that assumes properties of the model are properties of the world (e.g. that a boundary is natural, or that an aggregate describes a member).
  • Model drift — the gradual divergence of a model from its territory when it is updated by other models rather than by observation.
  • Triangulation — checking a claim against multiple independent maps; valid only when the maps do not share a common source.
  • Direct observation — contact with the territory itself; costly per unit, but the only input that can correct rather than rearrange a model.
