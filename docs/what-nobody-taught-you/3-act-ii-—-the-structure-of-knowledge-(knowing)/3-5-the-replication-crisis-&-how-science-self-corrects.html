3-5 The replication crisis & how science self-corrects

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Replication is science's error-correction routine: you run a study, someone else runs it again, and if the result holds up it becomes knowledge. The replication crisis is the discovery — made mostly in the 2010s, mostly in psychology, medicine, and economics — that this routine had quietly stopped running. When researchers finally went back and checked, a large fraction of published findings turned out not to hold. The interesting part isn't that scientists get things wrong; it's how the error accumulated, and how the same institutions that produced it are now repairing it.

What the crisis actually is

A "replication" means repeating a study and asking whether you reach the same conclusion. Starting around 2011, researchers began doing this at scale, and the results were consistent and uncomfortable:

  • Psychology (2015). A coordinated effort repeated 100 published studies from three top journals. About 36% produced a statistically significant result in the same direction; the average effect size was roughly half the originally reported one.
  • Cancer biology (2012). Of 53 landmark preclinical studies, 6 could be confirmed.
  • Economics (2016). Of 18 experimental studies from two leading journals, 11 replicated (61%).
  • Social science (2018). Of 21 papers published in Nature and Science, 13 replicated (62%).

Note the spread. Economics and high-profile social science fared much better than psychology, and preclinical cancer biology worst of all. Replication rates are not a single number describing "science"; they describe particular fields at a particular time, with particular habits.

Two definitions keep the discussion honest, because they are routinely conflated:

  • Reproducibility — same data, same code, same analysis, same answer. This is about whether anyone can check your arithmetic.
  • Replicability — new data, same protocol, same conclusion. This is about whether the finding is real.

A study can be perfectly reproducible (the analysis code runs cleanly) and completely unreplicable (the effect was noise).

Why it happens: incentives, not villains

The seductive explanation is fraud. It is mostly wrong. Outright fabrication exists, and paper mills have made it worse — more than 10,000 papers were retracted in a single year recently — but the bulk of the crisis is ordinary people responding rationally to ordinary incentives while using methods that permit too much freedom.

Five mechanisms drive it:

Analysis flexibility. Given one dataset, there are dozens of defensible choices — which outliers to drop, which covariates to include, whether to stop collecting now. If you try enough combinations, something will reach p < .05. The result isn't fabricated; it's the product of a garden of forking paths, and the researcher usually cannot tell you afterwards which fork they took.

Publication bias. Null results are hard to publish, so they go in a file drawer. The literature you read is the surviving half of the distribution — a survivorship problem applied to knowledge itself.

Low statistical power. Most psychology studies had enough subjects to detect a real effect maybe a third of the time. Underpowered studies don't merely fail to find real effects; they systematically overestimate the effects they do find, because only unusually large flukes clear the significance bar. The most dramatic result in a noisy field is usually the most exaggerated one.

Incentives. Careers, grants, and journals reward novelty and certainty, not careful understatement. A survey of more than 2,000 psychologists found that a majority admitted to at least one questionable practice — failing to report every measure collected, or deciding to collect more data after peeking at the results.

Base rates. Even a well-run study produces misleading evidence more often than intuition suggests, because of what Ioannidis pointed out in 2005: in a field where most hypotheses tested are false and the tests are weak, a "significant" result is more likely to be a false positive than a true one.

This is Goodhart's Law wearing a lab coat: once p < .05 became the target by which papers, jobs, and journals were measured, it stopped being a good measure of anything.

Why this is not an argument that science is broken

It is worth being precise about what has been discovered. The replication crisis was found by scientists, using scientific methods, and published in scientific journals. Meta-science — the study of how science itself performs — is now a field. The failure mode being exposed is not "the method doesn't work" but "the method was being run with weak statistics and perverse incentives, and it took institutional self-examination to find that out."

That is exactly what a self-correcting system is supposed to look like — late, expensive, and against the interests of the people who have to do the correcting.

The self-correction machinery

The repairs now underway are structural, not moral. They change what is rewarded and what is checkable.

  • Pre-registration and registered reports. Researchers post their hypothesis, sample size, and analysis plan before collecting data; some journals then accept or reject the paper on the plan, before results exist. This removes the flexibility that p-hacking requires, and it removes the publication penalty for a null result.
  • Multi-lab replication. Consortia run the same study simultaneously in dozens of laboratories, which separates a real effect from one lab's quirks and produces samples large enough to measure modest effects honestly.
  • Adversarial collaboration. Researchers who disagree design a study together, agreeing in advance what each outcome would mean. It's a genuinely clever move: it converts an argument into an experiment.
  • Open data, materials, and code. If anyone can re-run your analysis and see your raw measures, errors surface in months rather than never.
  • Meta-analysis and p-curve techniques. Pooling many studies, or examining the distribution of reported p-values, can reveal whether a literature looks like the byproduct of selective reporting.
  • Post-publication review. PubPeer and Retraction Watch have made it possible to challenge a paper publicly and permanently, independent of the journal that published it.
  • Statistical reform. Journals increasingly ask for effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than a binary significant/not-significant verdict, which discourages the illusion that a threshold settles a question.

The crucial bottleneck is the arrow from "effect shrinks" back into the literature. Correcting a published claim requires someone to fund, conduct, and publish a replication — activities that earn little credit. Failed replications are easier to publish than they were, but they are still nobody's path to tenure.

Worked example: "Feeling the Future"

In 2011, a distinguished social psychologist published a paper in a top journal reporting nine experiments showing that people's responses were influenced by events that had not happened yet. The effects were small but statistically significant, and the paper was methodologically conventional — nothing about it was obviously fraudulent.

Within a year, independent researchers ran the procedure with larger samples and found nothing. Statisticians reanalysed the original data and showed that the pattern was consistent with selective reporting rather than precognition. The finding is now treated as noise.

Look at what the cycle required: someone willing to run an unglamorous replication, a journal willing to publish a null result, and a field willing to accept that a well-published paper was wrong. It also required years, and the original paper still gets cited. That is the machinery working at realistic speed.

Where self-correction is slow or fails

Four honest limitations:

Correction lags citation. Retracted papers continue to be cited for years, and many practitioners never learn of the retraction. The literature has no automatic memory update — this is path dependence applied to knowledge.

"Failure to replicate" is itself ambiguous. A replication that fails may indicate a false original, a real effect that is smaller than reported, a real effect that only holds under boundary conditions (a different population, dose, or context), or simply an underpowered replication. The correct response to one failed replication is usually "this is now uncertain," not "this is false."

Fields vary enormously. Physics and chemistry have hard measurement standards, precise theory, and effects that are either there or not. Fields studying humans, with flexible measures and noisy outcomes, are where the crisis lives. Beware of anyone porting the finding from one to the other.

The fixes must be paid for. Pre-registration is cheap; multi-lab replication and long-term data sharing are not. And structural reform is slow because the people who would have to change are the people currently winning under the old rules.

What this means for you as a consumer of research

The practical rules are simple and mostly about not over-reading single results:

  • Trust distributions, not papers. One study is an anecdote with a methods section. A systematic review or meta-analysis is where a field's knowledge actually lives.
  • Prefer registered studies — especially anything labelled a registered report or a pre-registered replication. The plan existed before the result did, which rules out most of the ways a finding can be manufactured.
  • Look for the failed attempts. Search for the phenomenon name plus "failed replication." What you're looking for is whether anyone tried and what happened.
  • Watch the effect size, not the adjective. "Significant" means "unlikely under a null hypothesis," nothing more. A real but tiny effect and a large but fragile one both get printed as "linked to."
  • Expect shrinkage. In fields with flexible analysis, a finding's true magnitude is usually smaller than the headline. The bigger the claim, the more shrinkage to expect.
  • Ask what would change your mind, and then ask whether anyone has done it.

The broader lesson reaches past science. Any institution that produces knowledge — journalism, medicine, intelligence, your own memory — has some version of this problem: the outputs are shaped by what gets rewarded and published, not only by what is true. The unusual thing about science is that it contains a mechanism designed to catch itself. That mechanism is slow, expensive, and occasionally humiliating, which is precisely why it deserves more trust than any method that never has to apologize.

Terminology

Replication — Repeating a study to see whether its conclusion holds; the core test of whether a finding is real rather than a fluke.

Reproducibility — Obtaining the same result from the same data and analysis; distinct from replication, which requires new data.

Replication crisis — The large-scale discovery, from around 2011 onward, that many published findings in psychology, medicine, and related fields do not replicate.

p-hacking / selective reporting — Using analytic flexibility — dropping outliers, adding covariates, trying measures — until a desired significance threshold is crossed.

Garden of forking paths — Gelman's term for the many defensible analytic choices that produce spurious findings even when no deliberate p-hacking occurs.

HARKing — Hypothesizing After the Results are Known: presenting a post-hoc explanation as though it had been predicted.

Publication bias / file-drawer problem — The tendency for positive results to be published and null results to disappear, distorting the visible literature.

Statistical power — The probability a study will detect an effect that is actually there; low power both misses real effects and inflates the ones it finds.

Winner's curse — The tendency for the most extreme reported effects in a noisy field to be the least likely to hold up, because they cleared a significance threshold by luck.

Pre-registration — Publicly recording hypotheses, sample size, and analysis plan before data collection.

Registered report — A paper accepted or rejected on the basis of its pre-registered plan, with results published regardless of outcome; the strongest structural fix for publication bias.

Multi-lab replication — The same study run simultaneously by many laboratories to separate real effects from local quirks and to achieve adequate sample size.

Adversarial collaboration — A study designed jointly by researchers holding opposing views, with agreement in advance on what each possible result would mean.

Meta-analysis — Statistically pooling many studies to estimate an overall effect and expose patterns of selective reporting.

Retraction — A journal's formal withdrawal of a published paper; typically slow, and rarely accompanied by an update to everyone who has cited it.

Credibility revolution — The name for the reform movement — open data, pre-registration, replication, statistical reform — that emerged in response to the replication crisis.
