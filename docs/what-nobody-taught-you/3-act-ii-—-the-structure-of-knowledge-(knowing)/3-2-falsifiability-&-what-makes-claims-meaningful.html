3-2 Falsifiability & what makes claims meaningful

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Most of what people assert about the world comes with no specified failure condition. "Everything happens for a reason," "the market is driven by sentiment," "this hire just gets it" — each is phrased so that no possible observation could contradict it. Falsifiability is the habit of asking, before the evidence arrives, what would count against a claim. It is less a law of science than a sorting tool: it separates the claims that experience can settle from the ones it cannot, and it tells you which kind of conversation you are actually in.

The asymmetry of confirmation and refutation

Verification and refutation are not symmetrical, and this asymmetry is the engine of the whole idea. No finite number of confirming observations can establish a universal statement: however many white swans you count, the next one might be black. But a single counterexample destroys it. Karl Popper built his account of science on this point in The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934): theories are bold conjectures, and what makes a theory scientific is not that evidence supports it but that we can specify in advance the kind of event that would refute it.

The logic is modus tollens. If the hypothesis is true, then this observation should occur. The observation did not occur. Therefore the hypothesis is in trouble.

The corollary is that confirmations are cheap and mostly worthless. A prediction you could not have failed is not evidence of anything. "It will rain somewhere on Earth tomorrow" is nearly certain and nearly empty; "it will rain in Lisbon between noon and 2 p.m. tomorrow" is risky — and therefore informative when it holds. Popper's word for this is severity: a test is severe to the extent that it was likely to catch the claim out. This is why confirmation bias is not merely a moral failing — it is a structural one. If you go looking for support, you will find it, because support is abundant for almost any claim worth arguing about.

Worked example. Two commentators forecast the same tax cut. The first says it will boost growth "unless monetary conditions offset it, which they currently do." The second says corporate investment will rise at least 2% within eighteen months. Both sound like predictions. Only the second has a settlement date and a threshold, which means only the second can teach its author anything. The first will be "right" regardless of what happens, which is precisely why it is useless.

No hypothesis is an island

Here is where the story gets harder. Pierre Duhem (1906) and later W. V. O. Quine pointed out that hypotheses are never tested alone. What meets the data is always a bundle: the hypothesis, plus assumptions that the instrument works, that the sample is representative, that no confounding variable moved, that the background theories are roughly right. When a prediction fails, logic does not tell you which element of the bundle to blame. Any of them can absorb the damage.

Blaming an auxiliary is sometimes exactly right. When Uranus's orbit refused to match Newtonian predictions, Urbain Le Verrier did not discard gravity — he proposed an unseen planet and predicted where to look. Neptune was there. That is a rescue of the highest quality: the patch was specific, risky, and independently checkable.

The failure mode is the rescue that only absorbs the anomaly. "The polls were accurate; voters lied to pollsters." "The trial failed because the participants weren't motivated." "The strategy worked — the market just moved against us." Imre Lakatos framed this as the difference between progressive and degenerating research programmes: a programme is healthy when its patches generate new, testable predictions, and degenerating when patches do nothing but save the theory from the last result.

The plainest test for an ad hoc rescue is directional: would you have accepted this same excuse if the evidence had gone the other way? If the answer is no, the excuse is not part of your model — it is damage control.

Conspiracy theories are this failure mode taken to its limit. "The absence of evidence is evidence of the cover-up" makes every observation compatible with the claim. A structure that cannot lose is not a strong position; it is a claim that has quietly left the empirical game.

Falsifiable is not the same as meaningful

Popper's criterion was a demarcation between empirical science and non-science — not a test of meaning. It is worth being precise, because the distinction is routinely collapsed.

The logical positivists tried the stronger move: only statements verifiable by observation are meaningful. That programme collapsed partly on its own terms, since the verification principle cannot itself be verified. Meanwhile, entire categories of meaningful claims are unfalsifiable by nature. Mathematical theorems are proved, not tested. Moral claims — "you ought to keep your promise" — cannot be refuted by any observation, because no observation entails an ought. Singular historical claims, legal judgments, and aesthetic ones are likewise not the kind of thing a counterexample overturns.

So "is this falsifiable?" is not the question "is this true?" or "does this matter?" It is the question "which kind of conversation are we having?" Much apparent disagreement about facts is really disagreement about values or identity, dressed in the grammar of empirical dispute. Detecting that shift is a large part of what the is/ought gap is for.

It also matters that unfalsifiable frameworks can be genuinely productive as heuristics — "supply and demand," "the unconscious," "selection pressure" generate testable hypotheses by the dozen. The error is not using the lens; it is presenting the lens as a finding.

Using it as a drill

Operationalize before you look. State the claim, the observation that would sink it, the threshold, and the deadline — and write it down while the prediction is still live, for the same reason you should write down decisions rather than trust your recall of them. A practical template:

  • Claim: the new onboarding flow is better.
  • Prediction: users exposed to flow B convert at least 1 percentage point higher than flow A.
  • Window: 30 days, 10,000 users.
  • Settlement: if the lift is under 1 point, we revert.

Contrast that with "our product is more delightful," which cannot lose, cannot be measured, and therefore cannot improve anything.

Statistical claims need a caveat. A probabilistic hypothesis is never cleanly refuted by one result; an unexpected outcome makes it less likely rather than dead. That is where the Bayesian machinery does the work, and where pre-registration and replication discipline come in — deciding the analysis before seeing the data is falsifiability's statistical form.

Two matching errors to avoid. The first is demanding falsifiability of claims that were never empirical, then declaring victory — a cheap rhetorical move, and a category mistake. The second is the quiet one: holding a belief as though it were an empirical finding while refusing to name anything that would change your mind. If nothing could, you are not holding a belief. You are holding an identity.

Terminology

  • Falsifiability — the property of a claim such that some possible observation would count against it; Popper's criterion for demarcating empirical science from non-science.
  • Demarcation problem — the question of what distinguishes science from pseudo-science, as opposed to what distinguishes sense from nonsense.
  • Modus tollens — the valid inference form: if H then O; not O; therefore not H. The logical backbone of refutation.
  • Severity (of a test) — how likely a test was to expose the claim as false; a test you could not have failed provides no evidence.
  • Risky prediction — a specific, improbable-in-advance forecast whose success genuinely supports the theory that generated it.
  • Duhem–Quine problem — the underdetermination of theory by evidence: hypotheses are tested in bundles with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction does not identify the culprit.
  • Auxiliary hypothesis — a background assumption bundled with the main claim during testing; revising one can legitimately save a theory, or illegitimately insulate it.
  • Ad hoc rescue (immunizing stratagem) — a patch that absorbs a disconfirming result without generating any new testable prediction.
  • Progressive vs. degenerating programme (Lakatos) — a research programme is progressive when its modifications yield novel predictions and degenerating when they merely protect the core.
  • Underdetermination — the situation in which the same evidence is compatible with more than one theory; the reason evidence alone rarely forces a unique conclusion.
