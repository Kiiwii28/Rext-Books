3-6 Is-ought gap

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Every argument for what we should do contains something that was not in any argument about what is. That observation, made in passing by David Hume in 1739, turns out to be one of the most practically useful distinctions in this book — not because it forbids reasoning about ethics, but because it tells you exactly where reasoning about ethics must be checked. The is/ought gap is less a wall between facts and values than a demand for bookkeeping: if a conclusion tells you to act, at least one of your premises must already have told you what was worth wanting.

The gap, stated precisely

Hume's remark is worth quoting, because it is more careful than the slogan it became:

In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remark'd, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning, and establishes the being of a God, or makes observations concerning human affairs; when of a sudden I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not... (Treatise of Human Nature, 3.1.1)

His complaint is about a silent transition. The argument runs along on descriptive premises — how the world is — and then, without announcement, delivers a prescriptive conclusion. Hume's point is that this move needs a licence, and the licence was never shown.

Stated as logic rather than as complaint: from premises containing only descriptive terms, no conclusion containing an evaluative "ought" follows validly. You can derive oughts from oughts, and is-statements from is-statements; you cannot get from one to the other for free. Nothing here says moral claims are irrational, arbitrary, or merely emotional — Hume himself thought moral sentiment was a natural and motivating part of human psychology. The claim is narrow and formal: check the premises, because the conclusion contains something the premises did not.

The bridge premise is where all the interesting disagreement lives, and it is almost always the premise left silent. That silence is not usually dishonesty. It is that the speaker's values feel like facts to them — self-evident, universal, not worth stating.

What the gap does not say

Three misreadings are common enough to be worth disarming.

It is not "values are irrational." Logical entailment is one relation among many. Values can be argued for by appeal to consistency, to consequences, to coherence with other values, to what you'd accept behind a veil of ignorance, or to their track record in producing lives people endorse. None of that is entailment from description, and none of it is nothing.

It is not "facts and values never mix." Philippa Foot, Elizabeth Anscombe, and Alasdair MacIntyre all pointed out that many concepts are thick: "cruel," "cowardly," "brave," "honest," "generous" simultaneously describe and evaluate. To identify an act as cruel is already, in part, to appraise it. Anthropologists, evaluators, and doctors cannot do their jobs with only thin, purely descriptive terms. So the gap is not a clean boundary between two territories; it is a boundary that is easy to cross, hard to notice crossing, and worth noticing. A functional concept — "a knife," "a heart," "a farmer" — carries built-in standards, which is why the gap looks narrower in Aristotle's framework than in Hume's.

It is not the naturalistic fallacy. G. E. Moore's target was different: the claim that "good" can be defined as some natural property (pleasure, evolutionary fitness, what we desire to desire). His open-question argument — for any proposed definition, it remains an open question whether that property is actually good — is a claim about definition, not about inference. The two are routinely conflated. Keep them apart: Hume's gap is about derivation, Moore's is about meaning.

The reverse smuggling

The traffic runs both ways, and the reverse direction is just as common and more seductive. Moralistic fallacy: inferring what is from what ought to be. If the world should be fair, we begin to perceive it as fairer than it is; if a policy should work, its failures get reinterpreted as implementation problems rather than disconfirmation. Every ideology does this, and so does every well-run team protecting a favoured project.

The practical damage is that it corrupts the empirical side of the argument, which is the side you can actually settle. The is/ought gap tells you the value premise needs defending; the moralistic fallacy is what happens when, rather than defending it, you quietly rewrite the evidence to agree with it.

Using the distinction

The gap is a diagnostic tool with two main uses.

The premise audit. When someone (including you) says "the evidence says we should…", stop and locate the bridge. Not to refute the argument — usually to complete it, so that the actual disagreement becomes visible. Group disagreements that look like fights about data are often fights about values wearing a lab coat; conversely, loud value fights sometimes dissolve into a shared value plus an unresolvable empirical question. Separating the two tells you whether to go get more evidence or to go negotiate.

The honesty check. Naming your own bridge premise in advance is a strange kind of freedom. "I think we should fund this because I weight the next fifty years heavily" is a defensible position that can be argued with. "The data leave no choice" is not a position at all; it is a claim of exemption from reasoning.

Worked example

A hospital committee is deciding whether to keep a costly treatment available to a small number of very sick patients.

  • Is-claims: the treatment costs $2.1M per year, extends median survival by 11 months in a specific subgroup, and would need to be cut to fund a new emergency-department triage system projected to save an estimated 6 lives annually.
  • Bridge premises, unstated: that scarce resources should go where they save the most lives; that saved lives are comparable units; that 11 months of life is worth less than an expected full life; that aggregate lives saved should outrank identifiable patients; that the committee has the authority to make this trade.

Notice how many bridges there are, how plausible each is individually, and how contested each becomes when stated. The committee is not choosing between evidence and no evidence. It is choosing between value frameworks, using evidence to inform each. Making that explicit doesn't produce agreement — but it moves the argument from a dispute about facts, which cannot be won, to one about priorities, which at least can be conducted.

This connects directly to the veil-of-ignorance construction and to moral luck: much of what feels like a difference in moral perception is a difference in which premises you find obvious. It also connects to falsifiability. Value claims cannot be tested against the world the way a mechanism can, but they can be tested for consistency across cases, for the costs they impose, and for whether you would accept them applied to you. That is not science, but it is not arbitrary either — and it is the only terrain on which the normative half of any decision can honestly be argued.

Terminology

Is/ought gap (Hume's guillotine) — the thesis that no valid inference can move from premises containing only descriptive statements to a conclusion containing an evaluative "ought," unless a normative premise is smuggled in.

Descriptive statement — a claim about what is the case, in principle checkable against the world.

Normative statement — a claim about what should be the case, or about what is good, right, or worth doing.

Bridge premise — the unstated normative premise that licenses the move from facts to a prescription; the real locus of disagreement.

Validity (logical) — the property of an argument whose conclusion follows from its premises regardless of whether those premises are true. The gap is a claim about validity, not about truth.

Naturalistic fallacy — Moore's charge that "good" cannot be defined in terms of any natural property; distinct from, and often confused with, the is/ought gap.

Open-question argument — Moore's test for such definitions: for any proposed natural property, it remains an open question whether that property is good.

Thick and thin ethical concepts — thick concepts (cruel, brave, honest) describe and evaluate at once; thin ones (good, right) evaluate without describing. The existence of thick concepts blurs but does not erase the fact/value boundary.

Moralistic fallacy — inferring what is from what ought to be; the reverse smuggling, which corrupts evidence in favour of a preferred value.

Premise audit — the practice of identifying every bridge premise in an argument before evaluating the argument, so empirical and normative disputes can be separated and addressed on their own terms.
