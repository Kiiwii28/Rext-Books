3-3 Bayesian reasoning

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Bayes' theorem is a two-line piece of algebra that most people can derive and almost nobody applies. That gap is the point of this section. The formula tells you how to combine what you already believed with what you just observed; the difficulty is not the arithmetic but the discipline of admitting that you had a prior at all, that it was probably wrong, and that new evidence rarely moves it as far as it feels like it should.

The one-sentence version

Your new belief should be your old belief, reweighted by how much more likely the evidence you just saw would be if you were right than if you were wrong.

Everything else is bookkeeping. Written out, the odds form of Bayes' rule is:

  • prior odds × likelihood ratio = posterior odds

The prior odds express what you thought before. The likelihood ratio (LR) is the strength of the evidence — specifically, P(evidence | hypothesis) ÷ P(evidence | alternative). The posterior odds are what you should think now. Odds convert to probability with p = o / (1 + o), and back with o = p / (1 − p). Working in odds rather than probabilities is not a stylistic choice: it makes the update a multiplication, which is why the whole thing can be done in your head.

The two dials

The prior is not optional. A common misunderstanding is that Bayesian reasoning means approaching questions with an open mind. It does not. It means being explicit about the belief you already hold, because if you don't state a prior you still have one — it is just hidden, and the hidden default is usually either 50/50 (I have no idea) or 100% (I am certain). Both are strong claims, and neither survives contact with evidence particularly well.

The likelihood ratio is where the work happens. The useful question about any piece of evidence is not "how consistent is this with my view?" — almost everything is consistent with almost everything — but "how much more likely is this under my view than under the alternative?" Evidence that is equally likely either way has an LR of 1 and tells you nothing, no matter how vivid or personally satisfying it is. This one question eliminates a startling amount of bad reasoning.

Worked example: the screening test

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A test for it is 90% sensitive (it catches 90% of true cases) and has a 5% false positive rate (it flags 5% of healthy people). You test positive. What is the probability you have the disease?

The intuitive answer is "about 90%" — the number the test advertises. The correct answer is about 15%.

Run it in natural frequencies, imagining 1,000 people:

Has disease Healthy Total
Tests positive 9 ~50 ~59
Tests negative 1 ~940 ~941
Total 10 990 1,000

Of the ~59 positive tests, only 9 come from genuinely sick people. Nine out of fifty-nine is roughly 15%.

In odds form, the same calculation:

  • Prior odds = 1 : 99
  • Likelihood ratio = 0.90 / 0.05 = 18
  • Posterior odds = 18 : 99 ≈ 1 : 5.5, so p ≈ 1/6.5 ≈ 15%

Notice what the LR of 18 did: a genuinely strong test pushed a rare hypothesis from 1% to 15% — a fifteen-fold increase in odds, and still far from a diagnosis. This is the arithmetic behind a claim you will hear in clinics, in courts, and in risk departments constantly: a positive result on a good test for a rare condition usually means you probably don't have it. The test is fine. The rarity is doing the work.

Worked example: the impressive anecdote

A colleague reports that a new hire "solved a bug that had stumped the team for a week, in ten minutes." Does this mean she's exceptional?

Set the prior. In a typical hiring pipeline, maybe 10% of hires are genuinely exceptional (odds 1 : 9). Now the LR: how much more likely is a ten-minute fix under "exceptional" than under "competent and happened to have seen this exact bug class before"? Perhaps 3 : 1 — she is more likely to do it, but a lucky pattern match is a live possibility, and one anecdote is a single draw. Posterior odds = 3 : 9 = 1 : 3, or a 25% chance.

That is a real update — from 10% to 25% — and it is also nowhere near a conclusion. The lesson is not to discount the observation, but to see how many such observations would be needed. At an LR of 3 per anecdote, four independent anecdotes get you to roughly 3⁴ : 9 = 81 : 9 = 90%. One anecdote is a hint. Four are an argument. Priors are moved by accumulation, and the mind's error is to treat the first vivid data point as if it carried the weight of all four.

What goes wrong

Base rate neglect. The screening example is the canonical case: the prior (1%) gets ignored and the test characteristic (90%) is treated as the answer. This is common enough to have its own section later in this act — the outside view is just Bayes done with a properly sourced prior.

The transposed conditional. P(positive | disease) is not P(disease | positive). The prosecutor's fallacy — "there's a 1-in-a-million chance of this DNA match occurring at random, therefore the chance of innocence is 1 in a million" — is exactly this error, and it has convicted people. Whenever you hear a conditional probability quoted, check which way round it points.

Assuming a 50/50 prior. "I'll start with no assumptions" sounds neutral and is actually a strong, arbitrary claim. If you are estimating whether a given startup will succeed, the base rate for startups is not 50%.

Multiplying evidence that isn't independent. LRs combine by multiplication only when the pieces of evidence are independent given the hypothesis. Three colleagues saying the new hire is brilliant, after discussing it with each other, are closer to one piece of evidence than three. Correlated evidence is the most common way confident people end up wrong.

Priors that can't move. An LR of 18 applied to prior odds of 1 : 10,000 still leaves you at 1 : 555. Strong priors are not a bug — they are what protects you from every plausible-sounding crank — but they mean that evidence has to be very strong to change your mind, and that a mind which treats every disconfirming observation as a coincidence has effectively set its prior to infinity. Naming in advance what evidence would move you is the only audit that catches this.

Habits that make it work

  • Ask the LR question out loud. "How much more likely is this if I'm right than if I'm wrong?" You will often discover the answer is "about equally," which is the most useful verdict available.
  • Use natural frequencies. "Out of 1,000 people…" is far easier to compute correctly than percentages, and it makes the base rate impossible to skip.
  • Update in small steps rather than jumping to certainty. A 3 : 1 update applied five times is a huge change; applied once, it's a nudge. Most real inference is a sequence of nudges.
  • Notice silence. An absent observation is evidence when the observation would have occurred under the alternative. A detector that would certainly have fired if the hypothesis were true, and didn't, has a large LR against it. This is where Bayesian reasoning meets falsifiability: a claim that predicts nothing is a claim no evidence can move.
  • Keep score. Stated probabilities can be scored; hedged ones cannot. Updating is only a skill if you find out whether your updates were the right size, which is where calibrated confidence comes in.

One caveat keeps this honest: Bayesian reasoning is a normative standard — how a perfectly coherent reasoner would update — not a description of what brains do. People are demonstrably bad at it in the laboratory. But the predictive-processing account from Act I suggests the machinery is at least in the same family: perception itself behaves like a prior being corrected by prediction error, which is why the failure modes are so persistent. You are not learning a foreign language here. You are learning to do deliberately, with numbers, what your perceptual system does automatically and silently.

Terminology

  • Prior — the probability assigned to a hypothesis before new evidence is considered; expressed as odds when updating.
  • Likelihood — the probability of observing the evidence given a hypothesis; not the same as the probability of the hypothesis.
  • Likelihood ratio (LR) — P(evidence | hypothesis) ÷ P(evidence | alternative); the strength of the evidence, and the only quantity that does any updating work.
  • Posterior — the updated probability after combining prior and likelihood ratio; tomorrow's prior.
  • Odds form of Bayes' rule — prior odds × likelihood ratio = posterior odds; converts to probability via p = o / (1 + o).
  • Base rate — the prevalence of a hypothesis in a reference class; the usual source of a prior, and the thing most often ignored.
  • Base rate neglect — treating a test's accuracy as if it were the posterior probability, ignoring how rare the condition is.
  • Transposed conditional — confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A); the engine of the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • Prosecutor's fallacy — arguing that a small random-match probability equals a small probability of innocence.
  • Natural frequencies — restating probabilities as counts out of a concrete population (e.g. 9 of 59), which makes correct updating far easier.
  • Conditional independence — the assumption that pieces of evidence are unrelated given the hypothesis; required for multiplying likelihood ratios, and routinely violated.
  • Weight of evidence — the cumulative effect of many updates, conveniently measured by adding the logarithms of the likelihood ratios rather than multiplying them.
