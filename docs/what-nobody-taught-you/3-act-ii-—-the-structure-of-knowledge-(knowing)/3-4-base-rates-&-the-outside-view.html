3-4 Base rates & the outside view

Part of 3 Act II — The Structure of Knowledge (knowing)

Two people can look at the same decision and reach opposite conclusions without disagreeing about a single fact — because they started in different places. One reasons forward from the details of the case in front of them; the other starts from how often this kind of thing happens in general. The first is the inside view, the second the outside view, and the gap between them is one of the most reliable sources of error in ordinary judgment.

The question behind the question

Suppose someone asks: how likely is this? There is no way to answer without a reference class — a set of comparable cases whose frequency you can count. "How likely is this startup to succeed?" is unanswerable in the abstract; "what fraction of startups at this stage, in this sector, with this much funding, succeed?" is answerable. Probability is always probability relative to a class, and much of the skill lies in noticing that the class exists and choosing it well.

Base rate neglect is what happens when we skip that step. Instead of counting comparable cases, we reason causally from the vivid specifics: this founder is impressive, this plan is detailed, this project is different. Each claim may be true. The error is using that information instead of the base rate rather than in addition to it.

Base rate neglect: the screening test

The classic demonstration comes from medical testing. A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A screening test catches it 90% of the time when present (sensitivity), and gives a false positive 9% of the time when absent. Your test comes back positive. What is the chance you have the disease?

Most people — including, in Gigerenzer's studies, a large share of physicians — answer around 80–90%. The correct answer is about 9%.

The base rate is doing almost all the work, and it is invisible in the way the question is phrased. Lay it out for 1,000 people:

Test positive Test negative Total
Has disease 9 1 10
No disease 89 901 990
Total 98 902 1,000

Of 98 positive tests, 9 are true. The vast majority of positives are false alarms, because the disease is rare and even a good test generates a lot of noise across a large healthy population. Nothing about the test changed; the prevalence did. Run the same test in a clinic where 20% of patients have the disease and a positive result means something entirely different.

That last step is the odds form of Bayes' rule: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. The base rate supplies the prior; the evidence supplies the multiplier. Because a likelihood ratio is finite, evidence can only shift a base rate, never erase it — a point developed at length in the chapter on Bayesian reasoning.

The practical trick is to convert probabilities into natural frequencies — "9 out of 98" rather than "9% given a positive test" — and to draw the 1,000-person table. Conditional probabilities invite the transposed error: confusing P(positive | disease) with P(disease | positive). That swap has a name, the prosecutor's fallacy, and it has convicted people: a "1 in a million" DNA match means something very different when the pool of potential matches contains millions.

The outside view

Kahneman tells a story against himself. Running a workshop on judgment, he asked each member of his team to estimate how long their textbook project would take. Everyone gave roughly two years. He then asked a colleague with experience of such projects how long similar efforts had taken — and learned that around 40% of them were never finished at all, and those that were took seven to ten years. The team had detailed, sincere, internally coherent reasons for saying two years. The base rate said otherwise, and the base rate was right.

This is the planning fallacy: the systematic tendency to underestimate time, cost, and risk because we plan by imagining the specific sequence of steps going well, rather than by asking how often this kind of project finishes on schedule. It is not optimism about our abilities so much as a failure to consult the class. Flyvbjerg's studies of large infrastructure found average cost overruns on the order of 45% for rail projects, 34% for bridges and tunnels, and 20% for roads — a distribution so stable across countries and decades that it functions as a base rate you can plan against before you know anything about the specific project.

The discipline that follows is reference class forecasting: before estimating anything, find the distribution of outcomes for the class your case belongs to, and use that as your starting point. The class is usually available — cost overruns on construction, survival rates for restaurants, publication lag for journal articles, tenure rates for academic job searches, relapse rates for a treatment. What makes the outside view uncomfortable is precisely what makes it valuable: it tells you almost nothing about your case, which is the information you already have too much of.

Choosing the reference class

The outside view has an obvious soft spot. There is always more than one class you could belong to — this is the reference class problem, and it has no fully general solution. "Most novels sell fewer than 500 copies" and "most novels by authors with this agent and this advance sell more" are both true, and they disagree.

Three habits keep the choice honest:

  1. Start broad, then narrow only for reasons you could defend to a stranger. Narrow the class when you have a measurable, causally relevant difference — same sector, same stage, same regulatory regime — not because your case feels special. Everyone's case feels special; that feeling is the thing base rates exist to correct.
  2. Distrust classes built from survivors. "Most companies that made it did X" is not a base rate about X; it is a sample selected on the outcome. The class that matters includes the failures, and they are the ones that rarely leave data behind — which is the subject of the chapter on survivorship bias.
  3. Do not narrate the class away with one anecdote. A single vivid counterexample is evidence, but it is one data point; the base rate is thousands. The correct move is to ask how much better your case would have to be to beat the distribution, and whether you have any grounds for believing that.

When to override the base rate

The outside view is not a rule that says "always predict the average." It is a prior, and priors get updated. Three legitimate reasons to depart from a base rate:

  • You have information that is genuinely diagnostic. A particular hospital's infection rate, a named track record, a specific engineering assessment. Diagnostic means it would change the prediction for this case, not just feel reassuring.
  • The class has structurally changed. Base rates from an era before a technology, a regulation, or a market shift may simply not apply — the terrain of the black swan and fat-tailed chapter.
  • You are selecting on it. Deliberately filtering for the top of a distribution — picking only projects in the best decile — is a strategy, not base rate neglect. Just be honest about how you know you are in that decile.

A related and quietly powerful application is regression to the mean. Extreme outcomes are usually a mixture of skill and luck, and luck does not persist. The best predictor of next year's performance, absent other information, is not this year's — it is the class average. The outside view, in this sense, is simply the formal version of a rule most experienced people carry without naming: when something looks remarkable, ask what the unremarkable expectation was.

Where it bites

  • Forecasting and planning — schedules, budgets, and deadlines set from the inside view, systematically short.
  • Medicine — screening in low-prevalence populations, where most positives are noise.
  • Law and forensics — the prosecutor's fallacy; also why eyewitness confidence is a weak signal about accuracy.
  • Hiring and admissions — a strong interview is vivid, the base rate of job performance is dull, and the dull number predicts better.
  • Diagnosis of systems — "this team underperformed last quarter" may be regression to the mean rather than a problem to be fixed.
  • Your own beliefs — the outside view applied to a claim is simply asking what usually happens to claims of this type, which is a habit that overlaps heavily with calibration.

The discomfort is structural. The inside view is constructed, specific, and yours; the outside view is borrowed, coarse, and impersonal. That asymmetry is why the base rate is so often skipped, and why the person who reaches for it first so often turns out to be right.

Terminology

Base rate — The underlying frequency of an event or condition in a population, independent of the evidence at hand; the prior in a Bayesian update.

Reference class — The set of comparable cases used to establish a base rate; the choice of class largely determines the answer.

Outside view — Estimating by locating the case within a reference class and using that class's distribution of outcomes.

Inside view — Estimating by reasoning forward from the specific details of the case, ignoring the class.

Base rate neglect — The error of judging probability from case-specific evidence while ignoring the underlying frequency.

Transposed conditional / prosecutor's fallacy — Confusing P(evidence | hypothesis) with P(hypothesis | evidence), usually in legal or forensic reasoning.

Natural frequencies — Presenting probabilities as counts out of a concrete population ("9 in 98") rather than conditional percentages; markedly improves reasoning accuracy.

Likelihood ratio — The ratio of the probability of the evidence under one hypothesis versus another; the multiplier applied to prior odds in Bayes' rule.

Planning fallacy — The systematic underestimation of timelines, costs, and risks that results from planning from the inside view.

Reference class forecasting — A formal method that anchors an estimate in the outcome distribution of comparable projects before adjusting for specifics.

Reference class problem — The unresolved difficulty that any case belongs to many possible classes, which may yield conflicting base rates.

Regression to the mean — The statistical tendency for extreme outcomes to be followed by more average ones, absent a genuine change; the outside view applied across time.
