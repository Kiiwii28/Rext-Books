02-3 The Parts of a Company

Part of 02 Company

A company isn't a single blob of activity — it's a collection of parts, each doing a different job, wired together so the whole thing produces and sells something. Some of those parts are people in rooms, some are teams, some are whole buildings. But the parts exist in every company, even the ones where one person is doing all of them before breakfast.

Understanding the parts matters because most company problems aren't failures of effort. They're failures between the parts — the sales team promising a date that operations can't hit, or finance discovering at year-end what everyone else knew in March.

Three ways to slice the same company

You can carve a company up in more than one direction, and it's worth knowing which knife you're holding.

By ownership and control. Who owns the thing, who directs it, and who runs the day-to-day. That's the governance stack: owners at the top, a board or equivalent directing, executives executing. (The forms this takes — sole trader, partnership, limited company — belong to the "Types of Companies" section; here we only care that the layer exists.)

By function. What work actually gets done: making, selling, counting, hiring, buying. This is the layer most people mean when they say "the parts of a company."

By level. Who decides what. A front-line supervisor, a department head and a chief executive are all doing management, but at different altitudes.

For most of this section we'll stay with function, because it's the slice that explains how a company actually operates.

The governance parts: owns, directs, runs

Three questions, three parts.

  • Owners put capital in and expect a return. In a small firm that might be one person; in a large one it might be thousands of shareholders.
  • Directors are the part that sets direction and holds the executives accountable — the board. In a tiny company this is often just the owner wearing a different hat.
  • Executives are the part that runs the business day to day and turns direction into decisions.

These are parts, not necessarily people. In a two-person company, two people occupy all three. In a public company, they're three distinct groups, sometimes with real friction between them — which is the point of separating them.

The functional parts: what the company actually does

Every company has the same handful of functional parts, whether or not anyone has given them names.

Core parts touch the product or the customer directly:

  • Operations — makes the thing or delivers the service. The bakery's ovens, the software team's servers, the plumber's van.
  • Product and development — works out what to make next and how to make it better.
  • Sales and marketing — finds buyers and persuades them.

Support parts keep the core parts functioning:

  • Finance and accounting — tracks money in, money out, and what's actually left.
  • People (HR) — hires, trains, pays and looks after staff.
  • Procurement — buys what the company needs at a workable price.
  • Admin, legal and IT — the plumbing: contracts, systems, records, the building.

The split between core and support is useful but not sacred. In a bank, IT is core. In a bakery, it's a laptop and a card machine that someone sorts out on Sundays.

How the parts connect

Here's the thing that diagrams hide: the parts don't just sit in boxes. They hand work to each other in a sequence, and value gets added at each stop.

That loop is sometimes called the value chain, and the important word is handoff. Every arrow is a place where information can be lost, delayed, or quietly contradicted. When a company feels chaotic, it's usually not because a part is bad at its job — it's because two parts disagree about what the other one is doing.

A practical test: pick any two parts and ask what each one thinks the other has promised. If the answers differ, you've found a seam. Seams are where companies break.

Layers: the same parts, stacked

Alongside the functional split, companies stack into levels. The front line does the work and deals with customers; middle levels coordinate whole teams or sites; the top sets direction for everything. The earlier sections on management covered what each level does — the point here is that this is a third way of dividing the same company, cutting across the functional parts rather than replacing them.

Parts change with size, not with kind

A one-person business has all the functional parts. They just live in one head, and the handoffs take place inside someone's skull rather than across a meeting room. That's why solo founders get exhausted: they're running a company's worth of parts with no delegation.

As a company grows, the parts split apart and become visible. Someone becomes "the finance person." A department gets a name and a budget. Eventually the parts turn into real organisational units — and new support parts appear that didn't exist before, like a dedicated IT function or an internal legal team.

Two things drive the timing of that split:

  • Volume. When one person can no longer hold the work, it becomes a role.
  • Conflict. When two activities start pulling against each other — sales wanting speed, finance wanting control — they need to be separate parts with someone owning each side.

Worked example: Tandem Bikes

Maya starts a bike repair workshop alone. In head-count terms she's one person; in functional terms she's the whole diagram. She buys parts (procurement), fixes bikes (operations), posts on local forums (marketing), invoices customers (finance), and decides what services to offer (product).

At around eight customers a day she hits a ceiling. So she hires Tom. Now there's a real split: Maya does operations and product, Tom handles bookings, invoicing and ordering. Two parts, two people, and a new problem appears immediately — Tom quotes a two-day turnaround that Maya knows is really four. A seam, right on cue.

By year three Tandem has eleven staff and looks like the diagram: a workshop team, a small parts-and-stock function, one person on sales and marketing, and a bookkeeper two days a week. Maya spends her time on planning and hiring rather than fixing bikes. Nothing about the kind of parts changed. They just became visible, named, and staffed.

Then comes the classic growing pain: as the parts separate, information stops flowing automatically. Maya used to know every bike in the shop. Now she finds out about a delayed part from a customer complaint. Her fix isn't to work harder — it's to build a connection between parts that used to be a single person's memory: a simple job sheet everyone updates.

What this means in practice

  • A company is a set of parts plus the connections between them. Improving a part helps a little; improving the connections often helps a lot.
  • The parts exist even when they aren't named. If nobody is doing finance, finance is still happening — badly, and probably at 11 p.m.
  • Splitting parts creates seams. Every new hire or department adds a handoff. That's not a reason to avoid growth, it's a reason to be deliberate about it.
  • Parts get outsourced, and that's fine. A company can buy in its accounting, its IT or its manufacturing — an outside supplier becomes a de facto part of the business. But the handoff still needs managing.

Summary

  • A company can be sliced by ownership and control, by function, and by level — three views of the same thing.
  • The governance parts are owners, directors and executives; in small firms the same people fill all three.
  • The functional parts split into core (operations, product, sales and marketing) and support (finance, people, procurement, admin, legal, IT).
  • The parts are joined in a value chain of handoffs; most company chaos happens at these seams rather than inside a single part.
  • Every company has all the parts, even a one-person firm — growth simply makes them visible, named and staffed.
  • Splitting parts is driven by volume and by conflict between activities that pull in different directions.
  • Manage the connections, not just the boxes, and the company works.
