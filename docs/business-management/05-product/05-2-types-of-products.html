Types of Products

You know what a product is. The next problem is that "product" covers things that behave nothing alike. A chocolate bar and a factory lathe are both products, but they're bought by different people, for different reasons, at different speeds, and they leave wildly different amounts of money behind. Sorting products into types isn't a labelling exercise — the type tells you where to sell, what to charge, how to promote, and how much margin you can realistically expect.

Get the type wrong and you'll build a beautiful strategy for the wrong animal. Sell a wedding cake the way you sell a loaf of bread and you'll destroy the margin. Sell a loaf the way you sell a wedding cake and you'll lose the footfall.

The first cut: who is doing the buying?

The single most useful split is between consumer products (bought by individuals for personal use) and business products (bought by organisations to make or sell something of their own). It's sometimes labelled B2C and B2B.

The same physical object can be both. A kettle bought for a kitchen is a consumer product; a hundred kettles bought for a hotel chain are a business product. What changed isn't the kettle — it's the buyer's behaviour.

Consumer purchase Business purchase
Who buys An individual or household A company, school, hospital, government
Why To use or enjoy it To make, resell, or operate something
Typical volume One or two Many, often in bulk or on contract
Who decides Usually one person Often several — a buyer, a manager, a finance person
The decision Fast, often emotional, habit-driven Slower, more calculated, more paperwork
Where demand comes from Direct — "I want it" Derived — it depends on their customers

That last row is worth pausing on. Derived demand means the demand for a business product comes from the demand for whatever the business is making. A café buys more bread because more people are buying sandwiches. If the café's customers stop coming, the bread order stops too — and no amount of good salesmanship on your part changes it. This is the Market chapter's supply-and-demand machinery, one step further up the chain.

The four types of consumer product

Marketers have sorted consumer products by how hard the buyer thinks about the purchase. That one variable predicts almost everything else.

Convenience products are bought often, quickly, and with almost no thought. Milk, bread, chewing gum, a newspaper. The buyer's effort is tiny, so price sensitivity is high, and they'll happily swap to whatever's nearest or cheapest. That means your job is availability and habit, not persuasion: get into as many outlets as possible, keep the packaging instantly recognisable, and don't expect much of a price premium. Convenience goods tend to earn thin margins per unit and make their money on volume and repeat visits. This is why the Distribution and Stores chapters matter so much for them.

Shopping products are compared before buying. Shoes, a sofa, a laptop, a family holiday. The buyer actively gathers information, weighs price against quality against style, and often visits two or three places. Here differentiation earns its keep: better service, better design, a clearer guarantee, a nicer shop. You can charge more than the shop next door — but only by as much as the buyer thinks you're genuinely different. Remember the Types of Competition chapter: this is the world of monopolistic competition, where rivals are close substitutes rather than identical goods.

Specialty products are the ones the buyer will go out of their way for. A bespoke wedding cake, a particular camera lens, a specialist running shoe, a drug with no alternative. The buyer won't accept a substitute, so demand is inelastic — exactly the elasticity idea from the Market chapter. A modest price rise costs you very few sales. That's why specialty products carry the fattest margins and get sold through few, well-chosen outlets, where the brand and the expertise do the selling.

Unsought products are the awkward ones: the buyer isn't looking for them. Life insurance, a will, a fire extinguisher, a dental check-up, a funeral plan. Demand has to be created rather than captured, which makes selling expensive and trust essential. Notice that unsought isn't a comment on quality. Plenty of excellent products sit here. It's a comment on the buyer's state of mind when you reach them.

There's a second, quieter classification worth knowing: durable goods (used repeatedly over years — a fridge, a bike), non-durable goods (consumed quickly — a sandwich, shampoo) and services (intangible, and cannot be stored — a haircut, a repair, a night in a hotel). Services are perishable: an unsold seat on tonight's class is revenue you can never recover, which is why capacity planning and pricing-by-the-clock matter so much in service businesses.

How organisations buy

Business products sort differently, by what role they play in the buyer's operation.

Raw materials and parts go into the finished product. Flour for a bakery, steel for a carmaker, a gearbox bought from a supplier. Buyers care about two things above all: reliability of supply and price per unit, because these costs sit directly inside their own margin. This is the Raw Materials and Suppliers chapters in action — and it's why business buyers often sign long contracts rather than shop around each week.

Capital items are the big, long-lived purchases: buildings, ovens, delivery vans, IT systems. They're bought rarely, decided by committee, often financed with a loan, and written off over years as depreciation — which is the Financials chapter arriving through the back door. Because the sums are large and the decision is slow, capital goods are sold on negotiation, demonstration and after-sales support, not on a price tag.

Supplies and services keep the place running without becoming part of the product: cleaning, stationery, lightbulbs, legal advice, accountancy, IT support. These are routine and mostly chosen on convenience and trust, which is why they're so often outsourced to the same firm for years.

Two features tie all three together. Business buyers are fewer but much larger — one café contract may equal two hundred counter customers. And they're professional: they know their numbers, they ask for quotes, and they'll switch suppliers over a two pence difference if everything else is equal.

Other ways to slice the same product

A few more axes are worth keeping in your head, because each one changes the strategy.

  • Commodity vs differentiated. A commodity is interchangeable — nobody cares whose it is, so price is the only lever and margins get squeezed. A differentiated product has a reason to be chosen, which buys pricing power. Most businesses die trying to sell a commodity as if it were differentiated.
  • Physical vs digital. Digital products have almost no cost per extra copy, so extra sales drop straight to the bottom line. That's a completely different economics from baking, where every loaf costs flour.
  • Regulated vs unregulated. Alcohol, medicines, financial products and food all carry licences, labelling rules and extra taxes. The Regulations and Tax chapters are not background noise for these products — they shape the packaging and the price.
  • Branded vs own-label. The same factory output can be sold under a premium brand and under a supermarket's own label at half the price. Same product, different type in the buyer's mind.

Why the type changes your decisions

Pull it together and the classification stops being vocabulary and becomes a decision tool.

Type Where buyers expect it Price What sells it Margin
Convenience Everywhere, near the buyer Low, compared Packaging, habit, availability Thin per unit, volume-driven
Shopping A few good outlets Mid, compared Service, design, brand Medium
Specialty One exclusive place High Story, expertise, prestige Fat
Unsought Wherever you can reach them Varies Reassurance, selling effort Eaten by selling costs
Business products Negotiated, direct Contracted Reliability, support, price per unit Depends on scale

Read the columns together and you can see the trap. A convenience product sold through a single boutique will fail on availability. A specialty product discounted heavily "to drive volume" throws away the very thing that made it worth buying. The type sets the rules.

Worked example: Priya's whole range

Priya's bakery sells six things, and each one is a different type of product needing a different approach.

What she sells Product type What it demands from her
The daily white loaf at the counter Convenience Always in stock, cheap, no fuss. It brings people through the door.
Saturday sourdough Shopping Regulars compare it against other bakeries. Quality and reviews matter more than price.
Bespoke wedding cakes Specialty Booked months ahead, priced on trust, barely affected by rivals' prices.
Bread supplied to local cafés Business product Negotiated price, guaranteed volume, thin margin — and demand that lives or dies with the café's own trade.
Her Saturday bread-making class Service Only eight seats and cannot be stored, so an empty seat tonight is lost money forever.
Bagged "Priya's Bakery Flour" Convenience, in a commodity market Almost no pricing power. Competes on shelf position and the bakery's name.

Same ovens, same recipes, six different games. The loaf has to be cheap and everywhere; the wedding cake has to be expensive and rare; the café contract has to be reliable rather than exciting. If Priya managed all six the same way, four of them would lose money.

There's a management point here too. Her product mix — how many of each type she sells — is a planning decision, and checking how each one actually performs is the controlling half of the loop. Convenience goods supply the cash flow and the customers; specialty goods supply the margin; business contracts supply predictable volume. A good mix balances all three, and a bad mix looks busy while quietly losing money on half its range.

Two warnings

Buyer behaviour defines the type, not your intentions. You can't declare your sandwich a specialty product by pricing it like one. Speak to your own customers: do they compare you, or do they grab and go? Their behaviour, not your label, decides what works.

A product can change type. The thing that's specialty today becomes shopping when rivals copy it and convenience when everyone sells it. Types drift, usually in that direction, and the Life Cycle of a Product section picks up exactly where that drift comes from.

Summary

  • Products sort first by who buys them: consumer products (individuals, for personal use) or business products (organisations, to make or sell something).
  • Business demand is usually derived — it depends on the buyer's own customers, not on your sales effort.
  • Consumer products come in four types based on how hard the buyer thinks: convenience (fast, cheap, availability-driven), shopping (compared on price and quality), specialty (no substitute accepted, high margin), and unsought (the buyer isn't looking — demand must be created).
  • A parallel split runs by durability: durable goods, non-durable goods, and services, which are intangible and cannot be stored.
  • Business products split into raw materials and parts, capital items, and supplies and services — with very different buying cycles, contract lengths, and financial treatment.
  • Other useful axes: commodity vs differentiated, physical vs digital, regulated vs unregulated, branded vs own-label.
  • The type determines where you sell, what you charge, how you promote, and how fat your margin can be — so it sits underneath decisions in the Distribution, Stores, Revenue, Costs and Customer Service chapters.
  • Get the type right, and your whole mix earns its place: convenience brings footfall, specialty brings margin, business contracts bring volume.
