03-2 Types of Competition

Part of 03 Competitors

Not all competition feels the same. Competing with the bakery two doors down is one kind of fight; competing with the global coffee chain that opened a drive-through on the ring road is another; and if you happen to be the only plumber in a village of 400 people, the word "competition" barely applies at all.

What changes isn't just who you're up against — it's the structure of the market you're in. Structure decides how much power you have over price, how hard rivals have to fight, and how easily a newcomer can jump in and take a slice. Economists have a name for each shape, and once you know them you can place almost any business on the map.

The spectrum: from many to one

Markets run along a single line. At one end, lots of sellers offering essentially the same thing. At the other, a single seller with nobody to compete against.

Read it left to right as increasing power over price. On the left, no single seller can move the price — the market decides it. On the right, the seller more or less is the market.

Here's the whole landscape side by side:

Structure How many sellers The product Power over price Example
Perfect competition Very many Identical None Wheat, copper, foreign currency
Monopolistic competition Many Similar but differentiated A little Restaurants, salons, bakeries
Oligopoly A few big ones Similar or differentiated Considerable Airlines, mobile networks, soft drinks
Monopoly One No substitute Enormous The only water utility in a town

Two honest warnings before we go further. First, these are models, not boxes — real markets sit somewhere on the line, and a market can drift along it over years. Second, nobody actually runs a perfectly competitive market, in the literal sense. The concept is useful precisely because it's an extreme: it shows what happens to price when no seller has any leverage at all.

Perfect competition: nobody has any leverage

This is the textbook extreme. Picture wheat farming. There are thousands of growers. The grain from one farm is indistinguishable from the grain from the next. Buyers don't care whose it is, so they buy whichever is cheapest — which pushes everyone's price down to the same level.

Four things define it:

  • Many buyers and sellers, none big enough to matter.
  • A homogeneous product — identical, so nobody can charge a premium for theirs.
  • Full information — everyone knows the going price.
  • Easy entry and exit — if farming wheat becomes profitable, more people plant it, supply rises, and the price falls back.

The practical consequence is brutal and clarifying: in near-perfect competition, you cannot compete on price at all, because price is set for you. A wheat farmer's only real levers are cost per acre and yield. That's why commodity producers talk about efficiency the way other businesses talk about branding.

Most businesses aren't here. But parts of many businesses are — anything sold as a standard, swappable input sits close to this end.

Monopolistic competition: many rivals, each slightly different

Now move along the line. Still lots of sellers, but the products are no longer identical. This is where most small businesses live.

Think of the restaurants in a town centre. There might be forty of them, and they all sell food, but nobody would call them interchangeable. One is the cheap noodle place, one does birthdays, one has the good terrace, one is the only place open late.

Because each has something a bit distinctive — location, recipe, style, service — each has a small amount of pricing power. The noodle bar can charge £1 more than the place over the road and still fill up, because it isn't quite the same thing. But that power is limited: push too far and customers walk 30 metres to the alternative.

The key insight is the difference between substitutes and identical goods. Under monopolistic competition, rivals are close substitutes — good enough to steal your customers — but not so identical that price is all that matters. That's exactly the space where differentiation is worth the effort. Remember it; it's the entire reason branding, design and service exist as competitive weapons.

Oligopoly: a few giants, watching each other

Cut the number of sellers down to a handful and the whole game changes. This is an oligopoly: a market dominated by a small number of large firms.

Mobile networks, airlines, soft drinks, car manufacturers, breakfast cereals. In each case, three or four players hold most of the market.

The defining feature isn't just the small number — it's interdependence. Because there are so few of you, every move you make lands directly on your rivals, and every move they make lands on you. Cut your prices and they'll cut theirs within a week. Launch a new feature and they'll have a version out by spring. So oligopolists watch each other constantly, and the market often settles into one of two moods:

  • Price war — rivals slug it out on price and margins get shredded for everyone. Great for customers, miserable for profits.
  • Truce — rivals compete on anything but price: brand, features, service, loyalty schemes, advertising. This is why airlines compete on legroom and frequent-flyer points rather than on ticket price alone.

Two things make oligopolies sticky. High barriers to entry — building a mobile network or a car factory costs billions, so newcomers don't appear. And the risk of collusion: firms in a small market are tempted to agree prices with each other instead of competing, which is why most countries outlaw it. Keep it in mind: whenever you see a market with three or four dominant names and oddly similar prices, that's usually structure, not coincidence.

Monopoly: one seller, no substitute

At the far end sits the monopoly — a single seller with no close substitute. The only water utility in a town. The only railway line to the coast. The one company with a patented drug that treats a specific condition.

A monopolist has enormous power over price. There's nowhere else to go. That's precisely why monopolies attract regulators: left alone, a monopolist can charge what it likes, and customers have no defence.

Worth separating two flavours:

  • Natural monopoly — where one provider is genuinely more efficient than several. Laying two competing sets of water pipes under a city is absurd, so we accept one supplier and regulate what it can charge.
  • Created monopoly — where the position comes from patents, licences, exclusive contracts or a dominant brand rather than economics. These are the ones regulators watch most closely.

Practically, you rarely face a true monopoly as a competitor, because if you did, you wouldn't have a market to enter. But you may well face a near-monopoly locally — the one large supermarket in a small town, or the one supplier every customer already uses.

Beyond structure: price and non-price competition

Structure tells you how much pricing power you have. It doesn't tell you how rivals will actually fight. That comes in two flavours.

Price competition is the blunt instrument. Cut your price, win the sale, watch your margin disappear — and watch your rivals match you. It's most common where products are near-identical (near the left of our spectrum) and hardest to escape once started.

Non-price competition is everything else: better quality, faster delivery, friendlier service, stronger brand, more convenient location, a longer warranty. It's the standard strategy in differentiated and oligopoly markets, because it grows the value customers see without simply giving money away.

A useful habit: when you look at a rival, ask which of the two they're playing, and which one you're playing. Firms that muddle the two — advertising heavily and discounting constantly — usually end up with the costs of both and the benefits of neither.

The same bakery, four different markets

Priya's bakery sits in different structures depending on which slice of her business you look at — and that's normal.

  • The bread she sells at a farmers' market. Several stalls sell a very similar white loaf. Customers buy on price. If she charges 50p more, she sells nothing. That's near-perfect competition, and her only real lever is baking cheaper or better.
  • The bakery itself, in a town with a dozen cafés and bakeries. Her sourdough, her regulars, her corner plot. She can charge a bit more than the place up the road. That's monopolistic competition, and her differentiation is what earns the premium.
  • The wholesale contract to supply a supermarket chain. Three big bakeries bid for it, and they all price against each other. That's an oligopoly, complete with price-watching and long negotiations.
  • The village hall's Saturday market. Priya has a stall, but the hall only allows one bread seller. For those three hours, she's the only bread in the building — a small, temporary monopoly.

Nothing about Priya changed. The structure of each market she plays in did, and that structure dictated how she could compete in each one.

Why this matters to you

Market structure isn't academic trivia — it tells you what strategy is even available.

If your market looks like… Then competing on price… And your best move is…
Perfect competition Is a trap — everyone matches you Relentless cost and efficiency
Monopolistic competition Works short-term, erodes fast Sharpen what makes you different
Oligopoly Starts a war you'll all lose Compete on brand, features, service
Monopoly Isn't a thing — there's nobody to undercut Watch regulation and customer goodwill

The mistake people make is borrowing a strategy from the wrong row. Discounting hard in a market where all products are identical just destroys everyone's margins. Trying to win on brand alone in a commodity market where customers genuinely don't care which supplier they use is money set on fire.

Summary

  • Markets run along a spectrum from perfect competition (many sellers, identical products, no pricing power) to monopoly (one seller, no substitute, enormous pricing power).
  • In between sit monopolistic competition (many sellers, differentiated products, a little pricing power) and oligopoly (a few big sellers, heavily interdependent).
  • Two forces place you on that line: how many sellers there are and how similar their products are. Barriers to entry keep you there.
  • Oligopolies tend toward either price wars or a truce where rivals compete on everything except price. Small markets also tempt firms into collusion, which is usually illegal.
  • Monopolies — natural or created — attract regulators because customers have no alternative.
  • Rivals fight either on price (blunt, easily copied, destroys margin) or on non-price factors (quality, service, brand, convenience).
  • The same business can sit in several different structures at once, depending on which market you're looking at — and each one permits a different strategy.
