03-1 Who Your Competitors Are

Part of 03 Competitors

Most people, asked to name their competitors, will list the businesses that look exactly like theirs. That instinct is right, but it's only a fraction of the picture — and the fraction that gets business owners into trouble.

Start with a business you think you understand

Picture Priya's coffee shop, a small place on a busy corner. Ask her who her competitors are and she'll point at the café two doors down and the chain outlet by the station. Same product, same prices, same customers. Obvious.

Now watch a single customer walk in. Call her Sam. Sam has £8 in her pocket and half an hour free. She could spend it at Priya's — or at the café two doors down, yes. But she could also buy a sandwich at the deli, a smoothie from the juice bar, or a lottery ticket and a bag of crisps. She could make coffee at her desk upstairs. She could skip it entirely and save the money. Or she could spend nothing now and buy a cinema ticket on Friday.

Every one of those choices is money Priya doesn't get. And every one of them is, in some sense, competition.

Competition is for a decision, not a product category

That's the first big idea of this section, so let's say it plainly:

Your competitor is anyone who could take the sale you were hoping for — or could stop the customer from buying at all.

Not "anyone who sells coffee". Anyone who could redirect the customer's money, time, or attention away from you. That definition is wider than the one most owners use, and much more useful, because it points at things you can actually do something about.

Think of it as a contest for a place in someone's decision. Sam doesn't walk out of her office thinking "I shall now purchase a coffee." She thinks "I'm tired, I've got half an hour, what do I want?" Your business is one of several answers to that question. The other answers are your competitors — whether or not they look anything like you.

The simple test

Here's a question you can apply to any business, any time:

If the customer couldn't buy from me, who would they spend this money with instead — and what would have to be true for them to do it?

Whoever comes back as the answer is a competitor, no matter how different they seem. If the answer changes depending on the customer, the day, or the reason they're buying, then you have several sets of competitors — one for each situation.

Four different kinds of rival, and most businesses only watch the first column.

Direct competitors: the ones you already know

Direct competitors sell roughly the same thing, to roughly the same customer, in roughly the same way. The café two doors down is a direct competitor to Priya's. So is the chain by the station. So is the new place that just opened in the market hall, even though it's tastier and cheaper and you'd rather not think about it.

Why do these rivals matter so much? Because the customer experiences them as swaps. Sam doesn't feel like she's making a big decision — she picks whichever café is nearest, quietest, or has the pastries she likes. So you're not competing on grand strategy; you're competing on dozens of small, concrete things: the queue, the seats, the wifi, the loyalty card, whether the staff remember her order.

Direct competition is the easiest to spot and the easiest to copy. Everyone in your own industry is reading the same trade magazines and copying the same trends, which is why so many rival coffee shops feel interchangeable. Spotting these rivals isn't hard. The hard part is remembering they aren't the whole story.

Indirect competitors: different product, same need

An indirect competitor is a business that won't appear on any list of "businesses like mine", but which solves the same underlying need in a different way.

Sam's underlying need isn't coffee. It's more like "a pleasant fifteen minutes and a lift to get through the afternoon." Lots of things deliver that:

If Sam's need is… Direct option Indirect options
A lift in the afternoon Priya's coffee Energy drink, a walk, a nap, a chat with a colleague
Lunch Priya's sandwich The deli, the supermarket meal deal, the vending machine
Somewhere to work for an hour Priya's table The library, the co-working space, home
A treat Priya's cake Cinema, online shopping, a new phone case

Notice how the "indirect options" column contains businesses in completely different industries. The meal deal at the supermarket chain is not a coffee shop — but it competes for exactly the same £5 at midday, and it wins a lot of the time. The vending machine isn't glamorous, but it's right there, and convenience beats quality more often than anyone selling quality likes to admit.

The reason indirect competitors sneak up on people is that they don't look like threats. Nobody puts a supermarket's meal deal on a competitor analysis slide, right up until it eats a third of their lunch trade.

The third and fourth rivals: the customer, and nobody at all

Two more sets of rivals deserve names, because they're quietly huge.

The customer themselves. Sam could make coffee at her desk. A homeowner could paint their own walls instead of hiring a decorator. A firm could build its own software rather than buy yours — or just keep using a clunky spreadsheet. Whenever customers can supply themselves, that "competitor" is free, familiar, and always available. It sets a price ceiling on everything you do.

Doing nothing. This is the most underrated competitor in business. Sam could decide she doesn't need anything, and save her £8. Every marketing budget in the world is fighting not just rival firms but the customer's perfectly reasonable instinct to close their wallet and go back to their desk. A new business that frames its pitch as "how do we win against the other new businesses?" is often really fighting "why buy this at all?"

Where you sell changes who you're competing with

Here's a wrinkle worth internalising: your competitors change with your channel. The same product, sold a different way, faces a different set of rivals.

Suppose Priya starts selling her pastries wholesale to offices. She's no longer mostly competing with the café two doors down. Now she's up against the industrial bakery with the lower price and the reliable delivery, and the sandwich chain that already has a contract with those offices. Different channel, different rivals, different things that matter — price and reliability instead of atmosphere and seating.

This happens in every industry:

  • A bookshop competes with other bookshops on the high street, with supermarkets on price, and with a giant online retailer for the same purchase — and, increasingly, with Spotify and streaming services for the same evening hours.
  • A software firm competes with rival products feature-for-feature, and with "the client's own IT team" for the budget.
  • A farm shop competes with supermarkets in one direction and with other farm shops in the other.

The lesson: "who are my competitors?" has no single answer. It has an answer per customer, per channel, and per situation. Get into the habit of asking "in this particular fight, who else is bidding?"

The same company, several different fights

One more layer, then we'll stop. Rivals sit on two sides of you at once — the firms doing what you do, and the firms doing something else for the same need. But a single organisation can be in several fights simultaneously, and they rarely overlap.

Different fight, different rivals, different weapon. A business that tries to fight all four with one strategy — usually "be the best coffee in town" — will be mediocre at all of them and beaten at most.

There's also a dynamic version of this. Some rivals aren't competing with you yet. A supplier who could start making your product. A customer big enough to start making it themselves. A firm doing something adjacent that's a single decision away from being a direct competitor. These aren't hypotheticals — the biggest competitive shocks usually come from outside the usual suspect list.

Worked example: a bicycle shop down the road

Take a small independent bike shop, the sort Priya might pass on her way to work.

Asked who its competitors are, the owner says: "the two other bike shops in town, and online retailers for parts and accessories."

Run the test properly and the list gets longer and more interesting:

  • Other bike shops — direct rivals for repairs and fittings.
  • Online retailers — direct rivals, but competing mainly on price and range. The shop survives by being fast and by doing things a box in the post can't.
  • A national chain opening on the retail park — direct rival with a bigger range and cheaper labour.
  • The mobile mechanic van — a different business model, same need. Comes to the customer's house.
  • The customer's own shed — if a bike is easy enough to fix at home, that's a lost repair sale every time. (This is why some shops deliberately handle jobs customers can't do.)
  • A general "sports megastore" — indirect rival for "weekend money". It sells cheap bikes, sometimes badly.
  • Skipping the ride — the customer who stops cycling entirely hasn't been beaten by a rival; they've been beaten by inertia.
  • And increasingly, the electric scooter shop — nobody in the cycling trade considered it a rival five years ago.

The owner who only watches column one spends their energy matching the bike shop across the road on price, while the customer quietly takes their repair to the internet, their weekend money to the scooter, and their enthusiasm to the sofa. That's how businesses lose without ever apparently losing a fight.

Two things to be careful about

Don't make the net so wide it becomes useless. Technically, Priya competes with cinema tickets and new shoes for Sam's £8. True — but she can't do much about shoes, and treating every purchase on earth as a rival leads to paralysis. Use the wide view to notice threats; use the narrow view to act.

Directness is about the customer's perception, not your industry label. Two firms in the same industry code might rarely compete if they sell to completely different people at different prices. Two firms in unrelated industries might compete fiercely for the same pound. Trust the customer's decision, not the directory listings.

Summary

  • A competitor is anyone who could take the sale you wanted — or stop the customer buying at all. That's wider than "businesses like mine".
  • The test: if my customer couldn't buy from me, where would this money go instead?
  • Direct competitors sell roughly the same thing to roughly the same customer. Easiest to see, easiest to copy — and never the whole picture.
  • Indirect competitors solve the same need differently — the meal deal, the vending machine, the library table.
  • The customer themselves (doing it at home) and doing nothing at all are real rivals, and often the biggest ones.
  • Channel changes the competitive set: the same product sold wholesale faces different rivals from the same product sold over a counter.
  • One business can be in several separate fights at once, each with its own rivals and its own winning move.
  • Watch for rivals who aren't competing with you yet — suppliers, big customers, and firms one decision away from your market.
  • Keep the net wide enough to spot real threats, and narrow enough to still act.
